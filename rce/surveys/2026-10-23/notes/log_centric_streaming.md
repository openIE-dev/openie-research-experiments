## Key question 1: The "log is the database" lineage (Kreps, Kleppmann, event sourcing/CQRS, Helland, Lamport) — what was claimed and what practice found

### Takeaway
The lineage's claim is that an append-only ordered log of immutable facts plus deterministic replay is a complete database architecture; practice confirmed the model but found three recurring costs — unbounded history (fixed by snapshots and continue-as-new), event schema evolution (the first-ranked challenge in the only peer-reviewed field study found), and replay fragility when code changes. RCEdb already builds the fixes in (HS-9 fold, digest per sequence number) but has no stated event-schema-evolution rule, which is the lineage's largest unsolved practical problem.

### Cited Findings
- Kleppmann's 2015 Strange Loop talk framed the design as "a surreptitious attempt to take the database architecture we know, and turn it inside out", and argued a database as "an always-growing collection of immutable facts" is a more promising model than mutable shared state — [Kleppmann, Turning the database inside out (transcript, 4 March 2015)](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2015/03/04/turning-the-database-inside-out.html)
- A third-party textbook summarises the talk's materialised-view claim: materialised views are "not only being preprocessed data but also directly built from writes to the transaction log" — [O'Reilly, Streaming Databases, ch. 1](https://oreilly.com/library/view/streaming-databases/9781098154820/ch01.html); the expanded version is Kleppmann's 2016 report, whose abstract says it shows how to "reorient your database architecture around streams and materialized views" — [Kleppmann, Making Sense of Stream Processing (24 May 2016)](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2016/05/24/making-sense-of-stream-processing.html)
- Helland's "Immutability Changes Everything" is listed in ACM Queue vol. 13 issue 9 (DOI 10.1145/2844112); a 2015 meetup description quotes its thesis that "there is an inexorable trend towards storing and sending immutable data"; an O'Reilly book uses its line "The truth is the log" as an epigraph — [ACM Queue listing](https://researchr.org/journal/queue/issue/13/9); [Crossweb event page](https://crossweb.pl/en/events/immutability-changes-everything/); [O'Reilly, Designing Event-Driven Systems, Part I](https://oreilly.com/library/view/designing-event-driven-systems/9781492038252/part01.html). The CIDR 2015 venue named in the brief was not confirmed by search results this session (the Queue reprint was).
- Field study of event-sourced systems (grounded theory, 25 engineers): five challenge areas, with schema evolution listed first; five evolution tactics, recommending starting with versioned events and a weak schema, moving to upcasting and in some cases copy-and-transform — [Overeem, Spoor, Jansen, Brinkkemper, "An empirical characterization of event sourced systems and their schema evolution — lessons from industry", arXiv 2104.01146 (2021)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2104.01146); journal copy [Utrecht repository / JSS 2021](https://dspace.library.uu.nl/bitstream/1874/412217/1/1_s2.0_S0164121221000674_main.pdf)
- Practitioner guidance on snapshots: replay time versus storage trade-off; one 2026 guide gives illustrative (not benchmarked) figures of "roughly 2–5 seconds" to load a 50,000-event aggregate versus "under 20 ms" with a snapshot every 100 events — [OneUptime, How to implement event snapshotting (30 Jan 2026)](https://oneuptime.com/blog/post/2026-01-30-event-snapshotting/view). Treat the numbers as illustrative estimates.
- Anecdotal "event sourcing fails" write-ups (low weight; one appears to copy the other) describe the same incident: a `discount_code` field added to `OrderPlaced`, old projections ignoring it, a replay applying current discount logic to 2022 orders; the fix was upcasters plus versioned projections; other lessons listed: unbounded streams, feedback loops needing causation IDs and idempotency keys, GDPR deletion versus immutable history (pseudonymisation) — [Kitemetric, Event sourcing fails: 5 real-world lessons](https://kitemetric.com/blogs/event-sourcing-fails-5-real-world-lessons); [dev.to, When event sourcing fails](https://dev.to/alex_aslam/when-event-sourcing-fails-war-stories-from-production-1nk2)
- The shared-log lineage in systems research is listed by the Delos authors as CORFU (NSDI 2012), Tango (SOSP 2013), Hyder (SIGMOD 2015), CorfuDB, LogDevice, Scalog (NSDI 2020) — [Balakrishnan et al., OSDI 2020 slides](https://www.usenix.net/sites/default/files/conference/protected-files/osdi20_slides_balakrishnan.pdf)
- Tango's statement of the model: an object's state exists in two forms, "a history, which is an ordered sequence of updates stored durably in the shared log, and any number of views" in client RAM; the log "serves as the source of consistency, durability, and history" — [Balakrishnan et al., Tango, SOSP 2013 (PDF)](https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/mahesh/papers/tangososp.pdf)

### Inferences
- RCEdb's HS-9 ("state at k is the fold of D up to k") is the Tango/Kreps model stated as a law rather than an implementation; it is the mainstream position of this lineage, not a novelty. ADOPT (already adopted).
- The lineage's practical failures cluster on event schema evolution and code drift, not on the log itself. RCEdb's spec as summarised in the brief has no stated rule for versioned events or upcasters. Judgement: OPEN — RCEdb should state whether an event's schema is part of the hashed entry (which freezes it) and whether upcasting is a pure function applied at fold time (which keeps HS-7 rebuilds reproducible). Copy-and-transform (rewriting the log) is incompatible with a SHA-256 hash chain and should be REFUSED explicitly.
- GDPR-style deletion against an immutable hash-chained log is unsolved in the practitioner literature found; RCEdb should state a position (crypto-shredding of payloads with hashes retained is the common pattern but was not sourced this session). OPEN.

## Key question 2: Kafka and log stores — semantics, fsync policy, measured throughput, Jepsen data-loss analyses

### Takeaway
Kafka's durability rests on replication rather than per-write fsync (its own docs recommend disabling application fsync), its throughput and latency numbers are vendor-contested, and Jepsen found data loss in Redpanda 21.10.1 (2022) and unresolved write loss, aborted reads and torn transactions in the Kafka transaction protocol itself (2024). RCEdb's "host supplies only durable append" should be read as a requirement for fsync-before-ack that Kafka's defaults do not meet.

### Cited Findings
- Kafka's operations documentation: "We recommend using the default flush settings which disable application fsync entirely"; "durability in Kafka does not require syncing data to disk, as a failed node will always recover from its replicas"; on crash recovery Kafka checks CRCs for "any log segment not known to be fsync'd" and rebuilds the offset index — [Apache Kafka docs, Hardware and OS (0.11.0 page; same text across 0.10.x–2.0 pages)](https://kafka.apache.org/0110/operations/hardware-and-os/)
- Redpanda's engineering argument (vendor, competing design): losing unsynced data on one node can cause global data loss in a replicated system; Redpanda fsyncs before acknowledging acks=all — [Redpanda, Why fsync() (blog)](https://redpanda.com/blog/why-fsync-is-needed-for-data-safety-in-kafka-or-non-byzantine-protocols)
- Jepsen on Redpanda 21.10.1 (Kyle Kingsbury, 29 April 2022): idempotence required a server flag `enable_idempotence` even though the Java client logged it as enabled (fixed by default in 22.1.1); duplicate writes with idempotence enabled under single-node crash/pause/partition (issue #3039; dedup added in 21.10.2); inconsistent offsets — same message at multiple offsets, messages reordered to earlier offsets (issue #3003; fixed 21.10.3 "by waiting for the commit pointer to advance"); acknowledged messages that never appeared in any poll across 21.10.1–21.11.2 (Jepsen could not tell lost from delayed; still under investigation at publication) — [Jepsen: Redpanda 21.10.1](https://jepsen.io/analyses/redpanda-21.10.1); [PDF](https://jepsen.io/analyses/redpanda-21.10.1.pdf); [blog summary](https://jepsen.io/blog/2022-04-29-redpanda-21.10.1); Redpanda's own framing [here](https://www.redpanda.com/resources/redpandas-official-jepsen-report)
- Jepsen on Bufstream 0.1.0 (12 November 2024): three safety and two liveness issues including acknowledged writes lost in healthy clusters, resolved by 0.1.3; separately, "more serious problems in Kafka's transaction protocol, including write loss, aborted reads, and torn transactions" that affect Kafka, Bufstream and likely other Kafka-compatible systems and "remain unresolved"; the analysis also notes the producer default is acks=all and that acks=0 "may lose committed writes" — [Jepsen blog, Bufstream 0.1.0](https://jepsen.io/blog/2024-11-12-bufstream-0.1.0); [full analysis](https://jepsen.io/analyses/bufstream-0.1.0). Buf states the transaction flaw "will be addressed as part of KIP-890" — [Buf blog](https://buf.build/blog/bufstream-jepsen-report)
- KIP-500 replaces ZooKeeper with a Kafka-internal consensus layer; metadata is stored "in a partition inside Kafka rather than in ZooKeeper, with the controller acting as that partition's leader" — [Confluent, Apache Kafka Needs No Keeper](https://www.confluent.io/pt-br/blog/removing-zookeeper-dependency-in-kafka/); in Kafka 2.8 (2021) KRaft was "not production ready yet and should only be used for testing" — [Aiven blog on Kafka 2.8](https://aiven.io/blog/aiven-supports-apache-kafka-2-8)
- KIP-360 (Kafka 2.5, 2020): before it, when the log was truncated for retention the broker dropped producer state, causing UnknownProducerId errors; the fix keeps producer state until expiry — [Apache Kafka blog, What's new in 2.5.0](https://blogsarchive.apache.org/kafka/entry/what-s-new-in-apache2)
- Confluent's OpenMessaging-based benchmark (2020): Kafka peak 605 MB/s, Pulsar 305 MB/s, mirrored RabbitMQ 38 MB/s; at 200 MB/s load p99 latency 5 ms (Kafka) vs 25 ms (Pulsar) — [Confluent, Benchmarking Kafka vs Pulsar vs RabbitMQ](https://www.confluent.io/blog/kafka-fastest-messaging-system/)
- StreamNative's counter-benchmark (2020, updated 2022 with Pulsar 2.9.1 / Kafka 3.0): Pulsar 605 MB/s under the same durability guarantee; Pulsar p99 "about 5–15 ms" while Kafka's p99 "can go up to seconds" depending on topic count and durability — [StreamNative benchmark post](https://streamnative.io/blog/tech/2020-11-09-benchmark-pulsar-kafka-performance)
- Redpanda's benchmark (vendor): 1 KB messages, 16 producers/16 consumers, p99.999 measured; Kafka 3.2 tested to 1 GB/s; claims "at least 10x faster tail latencies than Apache Kafka—with up to 3x fewer nodes"; argues sustained >300 MB/s is impossible on AWS i3en.2xlarge — [Redpanda vs Kafka performance benchmark](https://redpanda.com/blog/redpanda-vs-kafka-performance-benchmark)
- Pulsar/BookKeeper: no Jepsen analysis was found; Jack Vanlightly reports formally verifying the BookKeeper protocol in TLA+ — [Vanlightly, Understanding how Apache Pulsar works (mirror)](https://ftpaii.notion.site/Understanding-How-Apache-Pulsar-Works-Jack-Vanlightly-da589d35f03743aabd4fa23eb9c4b3fb); a 2026 arXiv profiling paper locates Pulsar latency in BookKeeper's ForceWriteThread and journal fdatasync — [arXiv 2603.29113](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.29113)
- Vendor benchmarks disagree on Kafka latency (Confluent 5 ms p99 vs StreamNative "seconds" p99 vs Redpanda 10x claim); hardware, versions and durability settings differ. No neutral three-way benchmark was found.

### Inferences
- RCEdb's host contract ("durable append") is stronger than Kafka's default contract. Judgement: ADOPT an explicit definition that "durable" means the append is fsync'd (or replicated to a quorum that fsyncs) before the sync-result event is emitted; the Redpanda/Jepsen history shows that leaving this to a default silently weakens the log.
- Kafka's idempotent-producer and transaction machinery exists because the Kafka log is not content-addressed; duplicates and reordering are detectable only by sequence numbers per producer. RCEdb's per-sequence-number digest (HS-7) plus content hashing of {chain, seq, event, deps} makes duplicates and reorderings a hash mismatch rather than a protocol state problem. Judgement: ADOPT — but state that the sync-result event must carry the host's acknowledged seq and the entry hash, so a client can detect the Redpanda-style "same message at two offsets" anomaly.
- KIP-500 is evidence that even Kafka ended up storing its own metadata as a log partition; RCEdb's "indexes are folds of D" generalises that. No action.
- Kafka's unresolved transaction-protocol anomalies (2024) are a reason RCEdb should REFUSE multi-chain atomic transactions unless they are expressed as a single log entry (one event, one hash) or as a cut in the partial-order proposal; a two-phase protocol layered on top would inherit the same failure class. Judgement.
- Throughput targets: with vendor numbers in the 300–605 MB/s per cluster range and p99 in the 5–25 ms band under contested conditions, an estimate (mine) is that a single-writer SHA-256 hash-chained log with fsync per append will be bounded by storage fsync latency (sub-millisecond on NVMe to several ms on cloud disks) rather than hashing; no measurement of RCEdb exists to cite.

## Key question 3: Incremental view maintenance and streaming SQL (Materialize, DBSP/Feldera, Flink, RisingWave, Noria, ksqlDB) — determinism, out-of-order handling, published numbers

### Takeaway
DBSP (VLDB 2023 best paper, proofs machine-checked in Lean) gives a closed-form incrementalisation for any query built from Z-set operators, and Flo (POPL 2025) names the two semantic properties — streaming progress and eager execution — that make streaming outputs deterministic; both are a direct match for RCEdb's requirement that every index be a pure function of a log prefix. Published performance figures are vendor- or author-run (Noria 14M req/s on one server; Materialize "sub-10 ms"; Feldera "millions of events per second on a laptop") and none were independently reproduced in sources found.

### Cited Findings
- DBSP: Budiu, Chajed, McSherry, Ryzhyk, Tannen, "DBSP: Automatic Incremental View Maintenance for Rich Query Languages", PVLDB 16(7):1601–1614 (2023); a language for computations over streams plus "a general algorithm for incremental view maintenance over arbitrary DBSP programs"; SQL and Datalog can both be implemented in DBSP — [PVLDB PDF](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol16/p1601-budiu.pdf); [arXiv 2203.16684](https://arxiv.org/abs/2203.16684v1)
- The paper won the VLDB 2023 Best Paper award; Feldera states "all the proofs were machine checked using the Lean Theorem Prover" and that an open-source Rust implementation powers the Feldera engine — [Feldera blog, Best Research Paper VLDB 2023](https://feldera.com/blog/Best-Research-Paper-VLDB-2023)
- Later versions: a longer VLDB Journal version (April 2025) and a SIGMOD Research Highlights version (March 2024) — [Feldera docs, papers](https://docs.feldera.com/literature/papers/)
- Feldera product claims (vendor, not independently measured): evaluates "full SQL syntax and semantics completely incrementally" including joins, aggregates, correlated subqueries, window functions, recursive queries; "millions of events per second on a laptop" — [Feldera docs, Concepts](https://docs.feldera.com/concepts); [GitHub feldera/feldera](https://github.com/feldera/feldera)
- Flo (Laddad, Cheung, Hellerstein, Milano; POPL 2025, PACMPL 9, article 9; talk 24 January 2025): identifies "two general yet precise semantic properties: streaming progress and eager execution, which together ensure that streaming outputs are deterministic and kept fresh with respect to streaming inputs"; a type system separates bounded streams (operators may block on termination) from unbounded ones; the authors model Flink, LVars and DBSP in Flo and map each system's guarantees to the two properties — [arXiv 2411.08274](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2411.08274); [POPL 2025 listing](https://popl25.sigplan.org/details/POPL-2025-popl-research-papers/9/Flo-a-Semantic-Foundation-for-Progressive-Stream-Processing); follow-up "Stream programs are monoid homomorphisms with state" — [arXiv 2507.10799](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.10799)
- Noria (Gjengset et al., OSDI 2018): partially-stateful dataflow; authors report up to 14M requests/s on a single server, about 5x the load of hand-tuned MySQL queries, scaling to millions of writes and tens of millions of reads per second on EC2, and outperforming differential dataflow in their evaluation — [USENIX OSDI 2018, Noria](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/gjengset); [Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2018/10/29/noria-dynamic-partially-stateful-data-flow-for-high-performance-web-applications/)
- Materialize: built on Differential Dataflow (records carry +1/−1 diffs) over Timely Dataflow, whose progress tracking traces to Naiad (Microsoft Research, 2013); Materialize describes its answers as "the correct result on some specific (and recent) version of your data" and ties progress tracking to strict serializability; a vendor-hosted interview claims "sub-10 ms latency at scale"; a 2026 Materialize post claims its progress tracking is "orders of magnitude more efficient" than other stream processors — [Materialize architecture](https://materialize.com/blog/architecture); [Cockroach Labs interview](https://www.cockroachlabs.com/blog/how-frank-mcsherrys-company-achieves-sub-10ms-latency-at-scale/); [Materialize, Speeding up Timely Dataflow](https://materialize.com/blog/speeding-up-timely-dataflow/)
- RisingWave publishes a 2026 vendor benchmark against Materialize on Nexmark; figures not captured in search results and vendor-run — [RisingWave vs Materialize benchmark 2026](https://risingwave.com/blog/risingwave-vs-materialize-benchmark-2026)

### Inferences
- The DBSP proposal for RCEdb (integral/derivative operators define incremental indexes) is well founded: DBSP is exactly "index = Q applied to the integral of the change stream", and its incremental form is the formula in the brief, Q^Δ = D ∘ Q ∘ I. Judgement: ADOPT, with two conditions. (1) RCEdb's Z-set must be the bag semantics the brief says HS-8 states in the type; DBSP's Z-sets carry integer multiplicities, so a set-typed query is a Z-set with a `distinct` applied, which DBSP incrementalises non-linearly — the type must say which. (2) HS-7's "digest per sequence number" requires that the incremental fold and the from-scratch fold agree at every prefix; DBSP's theory guarantees this for the stream semantics (the chain rule is an equality, not an approximation), so the digest check becomes a runtime test of the implementation, not of the theory.
- Determinism: Materialize/Timely and DBSP are deterministic given a fixed input order and timestamps; Flink's output depends on watermark arrival (not sourced this session, see Gaps). RCEdb's HS-9 requires determinism per prefix of D, which rules out watermark-driven semantics unless the watermark is itself a log entry. Judgement: ADOPT Flo's framing — make "progress" an event in D (the sync-result event or an explicit cut entry) so that out-of-order arrival is resolved by the log order, never by wall-clock.
- Published throughput figures (Noria 14M req/s, Feldera "millions/s", Materialize sub-10 ms) are from authors or vendors and are not comparable; RCEdb should not quote them as targets. Estimate (mine): the binding constraint for RCEdb will be the single hash chain's append rate, not the incremental engine.

- The "7 million SQL Logic Tests" claim named in the brief could not be confirmed from any search result this session; the Feldera "Correctness at Feldera" page (https://www.feldera.com/blog/correctness-at-feldera) exists but was not fetchable. Do not cite the number without checking that page.

## Key question 4: Replay as the primary operation — Temporal/Cadence, Restate, Inngest, durable execution; what breaks replay

### Takeaway
Temporal caps a workflow's event history at 51,200 events / 50 MB (warnings at 10,240 / 10 MB; configurable self-hosted) and relies on continue-as-new and versioning markers (`getVersion`/patch) to survive code change; the documented failure mode is any nondeterministic decision in replayed code, including ones outside the patched branch. RCEdb avoids the user-code problem by construction (delta is total, deterministic, pure) but inherits the history-size problem, which only snapshots or cuts solve.

### Cited Findings
- Temporal event history limits: warning at 10,240 events or 10 MB, error at 51,200 events or 50 MB; "the limit is a precaution"; self-hosted values configurable via HistorySizeLimitError/Warn and HistoryCountLimit settings — [Temporal docs, Workflow Execution limits](https://docs.temporal.io/workflow-execution/limits); [self-hosted defaults](https://docs.temporal.io/self-hosted-guide/defaults.md); a community thread recommends continue-as-new over raising the limit — [community.temporal.io](https://community.temporal.io/t/increase-event-history-size-and-event-history-length/13027)
- Temporal Cloud adds a non-configurable 4 MB size limit per event-history transaction — [Temporal Cloud limits](https://docs.temporal.io/cloud/limits)
- Continue-As-New starts a new execution with a fresh history before the limit; SDKs expose e.g. `Workflow.getInfo().isContinueAsNewSuggested()` — [Temporal Ruby SDK, continue-as-new](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/ruby/continue-as-new)
- Versioning: a code change that alters the replayed command sequence (e.g. adding an activity) fails with a nondeterminism error; `Workflow.getVersion(changeId, DEFAULT_VERSION, maxVersion)` writes a marker event on first call so old executions take the old branch; for incompatible changes, register the workflow under a new name; old branches are removed once no executions remain — [Temporal Java versioning](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/java/workflows/versioning); [PHP versioning](https://docs.temporal.io/develop/php/workflows/versioning)
- The nondeterminism error can persist after adding `getVersion` when the nondeterminism sits outside the patched branch — in one case a timer duration computed from current time during worker-restart replay, with no marker in the old history — [Temporal community, Non-determinism after versioning](https://community.temporal.io/t/non-determinism-after-versioning/7527)
- Inngest uses step-level memoisation rather than whole-function journal replay: each step runs once, its result is persisted, later executions skip completed steps by injecting stored results; determinism/versioning rules are in a separate guide (not retrieved) — [Inngest docs, How functions are executed](https://www.inngest.com/docs/learn/how-functions-are-executed)
- Commentary on replay engines: orchestrator code "can't read the clock, pick random numbers or call outside services directly"; changing code while executions are in flight means "the journal no longer matches"; steps that timed out may have succeeded, so side effects must be idempotent — [ZenML, Why Kitaru doesn't use journal replay](https://www.zenml.io/blog/no-journal-replay); [Decagon glossary, durable execution](https://decagon.ai/glossary/what-is-durable-execution); [agnt5, Replay semantics explained](https://agnt5.com/blog/replay-semantics-explained/)

### Inferences
- Temporal's limits are per-workflow (one history), whereas RCEdb's D is one log for the whole program; the 50 MB figure is therefore not a direct analogue, but the mechanism (replay cost grows linearly with history; snapshot or continue-as-new is mandatory) is. Judgement: ADOPT a checkpoint/cut entry type in D (see key question 7) with an explicit policy for when the fold starts from a checkpoint; HS-9 must then be restated as "fold of D from the latest verified checkpoint", with the checkpoint's digest tying it to the full chain.
- Temporal's versioning markers are events in the history that record which code path was taken; this is the same move as RCEdb's hashing of the event with its chain metadata. Judgement: ADOPT a "program version" field in the entry hash or at each cut, so that replaying with a different delta is detected as a digest mismatch (HS-7) rather than silently producing a different state. Temporal shows that "the code is part of the history" is the only workable answer to drift.
- RCEdb's delta being total and pure removes the nondeterministic-user-code class entirely (no clock, no random, no I/O inside the step); this is a genuine advantage over Temporal, Restate and Inngest, where the rule is enforced only by convention. Judgement: the spec should make it a checked property (type-level or lint), since the Temporal community thread shows how easily conventions leak.
- Inngest's memoised-steps design is closer to RCEdb's "sync-result event" (the effect's result is recorded in the log and the step is never re-run) than to Temporal's replay. ADOPT the memoised-effect model, which the brief already implies.

### Gaps
- Restate's journal and its determinism limits were not sourced (search results covered Temporal and Inngest only).
- Cadence-specific limits were not separately confirmed; Temporal figures are assumed to descend from Cadence but this is not cited.

## Key question 5: Partial-order and causal logs — vector clocks, traces, Calvin, Tango/Corfu, Delos, FuzzyLog, DAG hash chains, CRDT op logs

### Takeaway
The shared-log research line went from a single totally-ordered log (CORFU/Tango) to a partially-ordered one (FuzzyLog, OSDI 2018: 3M single-key writes/s and 150K cross-shard atomic renames/s on 6 nodes) and to virtualised consensus (Delos, OSDI 2020, swapping protocols with a 10x latency gain and no downtime); RCEdb's 22 October 2026 partial-order proposal is in the FuzzyLog tradition and its theorem T62 is the standard "any consistent linearisation folds to the same cut state" result, but RCEdb's hash-by-{chain, seq, event, deps} is stricter than any of these systems, none of which were tamper-evident.

### Cited Findings
- FuzzyLog (Lockerman, Faleiro, Kim, Sankaran, Abadi, Aspnes, Sen, Balakrishnan; OSDI 2018, pp. 357–372): "a partially ordered shared log abstraction. Distributed applications can concurrently append to the partial order and play it back"; exposing the partial order "enables three key capabilities: linear scaling for throughput and capacity (without sacrificing atomicity), weaker consistency guarantees, and tolerance to network partitions"; implementation Dapple "stores the partial order compactly"; on 6 nodes a FuzzyLog-based ZooKeeper "supports 3M/sec single-key writes, and 150K/sec atomic cross-shard renames" — [USENIX OSDI 2018, FuzzyLog](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi18/presentation/lockerman); [PDF via MIT 6.824](https://pdos.csail.mit.edu/6.824/papers/fuzzylog.pdf); [Morning Paper summary](https://blog.acolyer.org/2018/11/02/the-fuzzylog-a-partially-ordered-shared-log/)
- Delos (Balakrishnan et al., OSDI 2020, Best Paper): virtualises the shared-log API into a reusable reconfiguration layer (VirtualLog) and pluggable ordering protocols (Loglets), which can be stacked and striped; Delos "reached production within 8 months, and 4 months later upgraded its consensus protocol without downtime for a 10X latency improvement" — [USENIX OSDI 2020, Delos](https://www.usenix.org/conference/osdi20/presentation/balakrishnan); [PDF](https://www.usenix.net/system/files/osdi20-balakrishnan.pdf); [Vanlightly introduction (Feb 2025)](https://jack-vanlightly.com/blog/2025/2/5/an-introduction-to-virtual-consensus-in-delos)
- Tango/CORFU: CORFU is "a global log which clients can append-to and read-from over a network"; Tango objects keep history in the log and views in client RAM — [Tango SOSP 2013 PDF](https://www.cs.yale.edu/homes/mahesh/papers/tangososp.pdf); vCorfu extends the model to distributed object stores — [vCorfu NSDI 2017](https://www.cs.princeton.edu/~mfreed/docs/vcorfu-nsdi17.pdf); Scalog was introduced to address CORFU's write-throughput limit — [Scalog NSDI 2020](https://www.usenix.net/system/files/nsdi20-paper-ding.pdf); one shared-log paper reports near-linear scaling "with additional sequencers, up to 3, as long as the read fraction of the workload is low" (search snippet, paper not identified precisely) — [Princeton talk listing](https://lists.cs.princeton.edu/pipermail/talks/2015-May/001270.html)

### Inferences
- The RCEdb proposal's entry hash over {chain, seq, event, deps} is a Merkle-DAG: each entry commits to its dependencies, so a fold over any linearisation can verify that every dep was folded first. FuzzyLog stores the partial order but does not hash it; Git/IPFS/Hypercore hash DAGs but do not fold them to relational state. RCEdb would be combining the two. Judgement: ADOPT, with the caveat that "seq" in a partial order must be defined per chain (a per-chain counter), otherwise seq and deps are redundant or contradictory; the brief's "state hashes only at cuts" is the right place to compare replicas, since per-entry state hashes are not well defined in a partial order (there is no single state after an entry).
- T62 (any linearisation consistent with deps folds to the same cut state) requires that concurrent (dep-unrelated) events commute under delta. This is the Mazurkiewicz-trace independence condition and the CALM monotonicity condition in another guise; it is a proof obligation on delta per pair of independent event types, not a free theorem. Judgement: OPEN — the spec should say whether T62 is proven for all delta or only when delta restricted to independent events is shown to commute (which is what FuzzyLog's "weaker consistency" admits by letting non-commuting ops observe different orders).
- Delos's lesson for RCEdb: keep the ordering protocol (host's durable append) behind the log API so it can be replaced; the brief's "host supplies only durable append and a sync-result event" already does this. ADOPT (already adopted).

### Gaps
- Calvin (deterministic transaction ordering via a sequencer layer), Hypercore (Merkle-tree append-only log, truncation), Git/IPFS DAG hashing and Automerge's op log were not sourced before the budget ran out; vector clocks and Mazurkiewicz traces likewise. The inference above that T62 needs commutativity of independent events is a judgement, not a citation.
- FuzzyLog's "weaker consistency" formal guarantee (what a client observes when playing back concurrent regions) is not quoted.

## Key question 6: CALM and coordination — Hellerstein & Alvaro, Anna, Bloom, Hydro; measured coordination cost; limits (Laddad 2022)

### Takeaway
CALM states that exactly the monotone programs have consistent coordination-free implementations; Anna's authors report competing KVSs spending up to 90% of time waiting for coordination and Anna achieving "over two orders of magnitude" speedup (self-benchmarked, high contention); Laddad et al. (PVLDB 2022) show CRDT guarantees cover updates only — observations of CRDT state are "unconstrained and unsafe" unless the query is monotone. RCEdb's "monotone rules and threshold reads are coordination-free" matches CALM exactly; the limit is that non-monotone reads (negation, aggregates, "is the set complete") require a cut.

### Cited Findings
- CALM theorem: "the programs that have consistent, coordination-free distributed implementations are exactly the programs that can be expressed in monotonic logic" — [Hellerstein & Alvaro, Keeping CALM (arXiv 1901.01930; CACM 2020)](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.01930); [NSF PAR copy](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10221254)
- Same paper: state-of-the-art multiprocessor KVSs "can spend 90% of their time waiting for coordination"; "a coordination-free implementation called Anna achieves over two orders of magnitude speedup" — [Keeping CALM](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1901.01930)
- Anna (Wu, Faleiro, Lin, Hellerstein; arXiv 1809.00089, 2018; Berkeley TR EECS-2019-122): Anna "spent over 90% of its compute cycles serving put and get requests, while state-of-the-art, competing systems were achieving less than 10% on high-contention workloads"; "outperformed the competition by orders of magnitude at many scale points"; consistency levels are built by "the monotone composition of simple lattice structures"; design is "partitioned, multi-mastered… wait-free execution and coordination-free consistency" — [arXiv 1809.00089](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1809.00089); [Berkeley TR EECS-2019-122](https://www2.eecs.berkeley.edu/Pubs/TechRpts/2019/EECS-2019-122.html); [RISELab blog](https://rise.cs.berkeley.edu/blog/anna-kvs). No independent replication of the two-orders-of-magnitude figure was found.
- Laddad, Power, Milano, Cheung, Crooks, Hellerstein, "Keep CALM and CRDT On", PVLDB 16(4):856–863 (2022; arXiv 23 Oct 2022): CRDT "guarantees extend only to data updates", "observations of CRDT state are unconstrained and unsafe"; the paper extends CRDTs with a query model that uses CALM monotonicity to decide which queries are safe without coordination — [PVLDB PDF](https://www.vldb.org/pvldb/vol16/p856-power.pdf); [arXiv 2210.12605](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.12605v1)
- Flo (POPL 2025) is listed under the Hydro project's publications — [Hydro at Princeton](https://cs.princeton.edu/~mpmilano/project/hydro)

### Inferences
- RCEdb's partial-order proposal ("monotone (CALM) rules and threshold reads coordination-free") is the Keep-CALM-and-CRDT-On result applied to a hash-chained log: a threshold read ("at least n events with property p have been seen") is monotone and may be answered from any prefix; a read that reports the current value of a non-monotone query is only safe at a cut. Judgement: ADOPT, and make the query type system (HS-8) record monotonicity so the engine can refuse to answer a non-monotone query without a cut.
- Anna's 90%/two-orders figure is for a high-contention multi-master KVS; RCEdb's single hash chain per writer is a different regime, so the figure is not a prediction for RCEdb. Estimate (mine): the measurable benefit in RCEdb is write availability across chains during partition, not per-op throughput.
- Lattice state (Anna, Bloom) and hash-chained events are compatible: a lattice merge is a monotone fold of a partial order. But a CRDT-style merge that discards the op (state-based CRDT) would break HS-9; only op-based (log-based) CRDTs fit. Judgement: REFUSE state-based CRDT replication as the primary mechanism; ADOPT op-based merges expressed as deps in D.

### Gaps
- Bloom/Dedalus and Hydro/Hydroflow (DFIR) details were not sourced before the budget ran out.
- No measurement of the cost of a "cut" (the coordination RCEdb retains) exists in any source found; this is RCEdb's own measurement to make.

## Key question 7: Snapshots and compaction with integrity — Merkle roots, checkpoint entries, Certificate Transparency / Trillian / QLDB

### Takeaway
The transparency-log pattern (RFC 6962) proves that a later log version is a superset of an earlier one with a Merkle consistency proof and detects split views by comparing tree heads; QLDB used the same construction (SHA-256, one block per transaction, hash-linked, Merkle-based digest and proofs) and was retired on 31 July 2025 with data not migrated deleted; Trillian is now in maintenance mode in favour of tiled logs (Tessera). RCEdb should add a checkpoint entry that commits to both the state digest and the Merkle root of the log so far, so that compaction can drop entries without breaking verifiability.

### Cited Findings
- RFC 6962 (experimental; now obsoleted by its successor): "Merkle Trees can be used to show that any particular version of the log is a superset of any particular previous version"; "if a log attempts to show different things to different people, this can be efficiently detected by comparing tree roots and consistency proofs" — [RFC 6962](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc6962)
- Trillian "is now in maintenance mode. The next generation of transparency logs uses Tiled APIs and are better supported by Tessera"; new log operators should try Tessera first — [google.github.io/trillian](https://google.github.io/trillian/)
- A Trillian GitHub issue cites a non-peer-reviewed alternative design claiming consistency proofs at "ca 95% of the size" (single commenter) — [trillian issue 3078](https://redirect.github.com/google/trillian/issues/3078)
- QLDB verification: "SHA-256 hash function with a Merkle-tree-based model to generate a cryptographic representation of your journal, called the digest"; one block per transaction, blocks hash-linked; a proof is "the ordered list of 256-bit hash values" from a revision to the digest; the docs motivate the Merkle structure by noting that "the larger your ledger grows, the less efficient it becomes to recompute the entire hash chain of the journal for verification" — [AWS QLDB developer guide, Data verification (FR locale)](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/fr_fr/qldb/latest/developerguide/verification.html)
- QLDB end of support: 31 July 2025; AWS recommends Aurora PostgreSQL; new ledgers blocked from the July 2024 announcement; data not migrated deleted after end of support — [Classmethod (announcement, July 2024)](https://dev.classmethod.jp/articles/amazon-qldb-eos-announcement/); [Codenotary (post-shutdown)](https://codenotary.com/blog/navigating-the-transition-from-amazon-qldb-to-immudb-vault); [AWS QLDB developer guide (FR), getting-started next steps](https://docs.aws.amazon.com/fr_fr/qldb/latest/developerguide/getting-started-next-steps.html)
- Temporal's continue-as-new (key question 4) is the non-cryptographic form of the same operation: start a new history whose first entry references the old one.

### Inferences
- A plain SHA-256 chain (each entry hashes its predecessor) lets a verifier check a full replay but gives no O(log n) proof that entry i is in the log as of head k, and no way to drop old entries without losing the ability to verify later ones from scratch. A Merkle tree over entries (RFC 6962 style) gives both inclusion and consistency proofs; QLDB's point about recomputing "the entire hash chain" is the operational reason. Judgement: ADOPT a dual commitment — keep the chain hash for append ordering and add a Merkle root at each checkpoint/cut entry; the checkpoint entry hashes {chain, seq, state_digest, merkle_root, prev_checkpoint}. A reader that holds a verified checkpoint can fold from its state digest and verify later entries without the dropped prefix, which is what "compaction while keeping verifiability" means.
- HS-7's "digest per sequence number" already produces the per-prefix state hashes; what is missing is the log-side Merkle commitment that makes a checkpoint auditable against the full history by a third party who retains it. OPEN whether RCEdb needs third-party auditability (CT's split-view detection) or only self-verification; if the former, the gossip of tree heads between replicas is a new protocol.
- QLDB's retirement (12 months' notice, deletion after cutoff) is a practical argument against depending on a hosted ledger; it says nothing against the design, which Aurora-with-audit replaced with a weaker guarantee. Judgement: no design implication; a sourcing implication (RCEdb's host is its own).
- The RFC 6962 "superset" proof assumes a totally ordered append-only log; for the partial-order proposal, consistency proofs need to be per chain, with cuts carrying one root per chain (or a root over sorted chain roots). OPEN.

### Gaps
- Trillian's signed tree head, checkpoint format (C2SP) and Tessera's tiled layout were not sourced before the budget ran out.
- Hypercore's truncation semantics and Git's handling of shallow clones (practical examples of compacting a hash DAG) were not sourced.
- No source was found that measures the cost of inclusion/consistency proofs at RCEdb-like scales; the O(log n) claim is standard but is stated here without a fetched citation.
