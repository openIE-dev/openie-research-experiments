# Working note 15: Performance portability of one unmodified source across CPU, GPU and FPGA (quantitative)

Raised by the completeness critic: The survey lists the portability frameworks (Kokkos, RAJA, SYCL, OpenCL, OpenMP, Halide and others) but not the quantitative performance-portability literature. It also has no evidence on whether GPU-style kernels have to be rewritten for FPGAs. For 'one program form' to settle the controversy, the synthesis has to say whether one source runs at acceptable efficiency on every fabric, or whether it only keeps one meaning while each fabric needs its own source. Vendor FPGA guides that require single-work-item kernels and channels suggest the second. No source in the notes supports either claim yet.

Written 24 October 2026 by a research agent with web search; github.com was reachable and most other hosts were not, so many sources were confirmed from search extracts, as each bullet says. Not independently re-verified claim by claim, unlike notes 7 and 8.

**Performance portability (PP) metric**
- PP of an application over a platform set H is the harmonic mean of its per-platform efficiencies when every platform in H is supported, and 0 if any platform is unsupported. Application efficiency is measured against the best known performance on that platform; architectural efficiency is measured against theoretical peak. The paper itself was not reached; this definition is taken from Intel's reference implementation, which cites it [secondhand]. Pennycook, S.J., Sewall, J.D., Lee, V.W., "A Metric for Performance Portability," PMBS 2016, arXiv:1611.07409, via `p3analysis/metrics/_pp.py` in https://github.com/intel/p3-analysis-library (commit f0a36c8, Jan 2026).
- The library's docs say the problem/application/platform and application-versus-architectural efficiency terms were first introduced in the 2019 paper. No PP values from that paper could be retrieved [secondhand]. Pennycook, Sewall, Lee, "Implications of a Metric for Performance Portability," Future Generation Computer Systems 92:947–958, 2019, doi:10.1016/j.future.2017.08.007, via https://github.com/intel/p3-analysis-library/blob/main/docs/source/analysis.rst
- The same library implements Harrell and Kitson's "code divergence": the mean pairwise Jaccard distance between the source each platform needs. This is the existing way to measure "same source" separately from "same semantics" [secondhand]. Harrell, S.L., Kitson, J., et al., "Effective Performance Portability," P3HPC 2018, doi:10.1109/P3HPC.2018.00006, via `p3analysis/metrics/_divergence.py`.
- Marowka's critiques (2021–2023) could not be retrieved, and nothing on GitHub covers them, so none of their content is reported. The response paper Pennycook, J.D. Sewall, "Revisiting a Metric for Performance Portability," doi:10.1109/P3HPC54578.2021.00004, is confirmed only as a citation entry in the P3 library's CITATION.md (which lists it under P3HPC 2020).

**CPU+GPU studies (Bristol/Intel datasets)**
- The P3HPC 2019 dataset covers 12 platforms (7 CPUs, counting NEC Aurora; GPUs K20, P100, V100, Turing, Radeon VII) and 5 models (OpenMP, Kokkos, CUDA, OpenACC, OpenCL). No FPGA is included. For BabelStream and CloverLeaf, no model runs on all 12 platforms, so every model's PP is 0 [derived]. Over only the platforms each model supports, BabelStream's harmonic mean is OpenMP 0.95 (11 platforms) and Kokkos 0.89 (10). CloverLeaf's is Kokkos 0.66 (10), OpenMP 0.35 (9; lowest 0.06 on K20) and OpenACC 0.62 (7) [derived]. Deakin, T. et al., "Performance Portability Across Diverse Computer Architectures," P3HPC 2019, doi:10.1109/P3HPC49587.2019.00006; data in https://github.com/UoB-HPC/performance-portability (`metrics/data/*.csv`, commit c855363, Jun 2021).
- TeaLeaf (11 platforms): OpenMP is the only model that runs everywhere, with PP 0.46 and a lowest efficiency of 0.12 [derived]. Measured against CUDA on four NVIDIA GPUs, Kokkos reaches 0.62–0.65 on TeaLeaf and 0.46/0.85/0.82/1.01 on CloverLeaf (K20/P100/V100/Turing). OpenMP reaches 0.12–0.65 on TeaLeaf [derived]. Same source and dataset as above.
- BabelStream 2020 (15 CPU/GPU platforms, including the Intel Iris Pro Gen9): only OpenMP has non-zero PP, at 0.90. Kokkos reaches 0.91 on 14 platforms, SYCL 0.57 on 14 (lowest 0.18), OpenCL 0.51 and OpenACC 0.33 on 11 [derived]. On NVIDIA GPUs Kokkos reaches 0.98–1.01 of CUDA and SYCL 0.92–0.99 [derived]. Deakin, T., Poenaru, A., Lin, T., McIntosh-Smith, S., "Tracking Performance Portability on the Yellow Brick Road to Exascale," P3HPC 2020, doi:10.1109/P3HPC51967.2020.00006; data as above.
- "Same model" is not always "same source". BabelStream's OpenMP kernels switch between `omp parallel for` (CPU) and `omp target teams distribute parallel for simd` (GPU) under `#ifdef OMP_TARGET_GPU`. CloverLeaf, TeaLeaf and miniBUDE each keep one source directory per model (`src/<model>`). None of these READMEs lists an FPGA model. Deakin, Price, Martineau, McIntosh-Smith, "Evaluating attainable memory bandwidth of parallel programming models via BabelStream," IJCSE 17(3):247–262, 2018, doi:10.1504/IJCSE.2018.095847; code at https://github.com/UoB-HPC/BabelStream (`src/omp/OMPStream.cpp`), https://github.com/UoB-HPC/CloverLeaf, https://github.com/UoB-HPC/miniBUDE (Poenaru, Lin, McIntosh-Smith, ISC 2021, pp. 332–350).

**FPGA results**
- In the Zohouri et al. FPGA port of Rodinia, version v0 is the "originally developed for GPUs" NDRange kernel with minimal changes and no optimization. v1/v3/v5 are single-work-item kernels, with v5 described as a "kernel rewrite" (shift registers, blocking, loop collapse, systolic arrays). The fastest variants are rewrites: single-work-item for NW (v5), Hotspot and Hotspot3D (v7, "complete kernel rewrite" plus temporal blocking), SRAD (v5) and CFD (v5, on Stratix V); a rewritten NDRange kernel for LUD (v4). The SC16 paper's speedup numbers could not be retrieved. Zohouri, H.R., Maruyama, N., Smith, A., Matsuda, M., Matsuoka, S., "Evaluating and Optimizing OpenCL Kernels for High Performance Computing with FPGAs," SC16, https://dl.acm.org/citation.cfm?id=3014951; variant notes in https://github.com/fpga-opencl-benchmarks/rodinia_fpga (`opencl/*/README_fpga.md` and the wiki).
- Altis-SYCL is the only verified study that runs one SYCL source on CPUs (Xeon 6128, EPYC 7763), GPUs (RTX 2080, A100, Intel P630) and an FPGA (Stratix 10). That source was migrated from CUDA with Intel's DPC++ Compatibility Tool (DPCT) and tuned for the RTX 2080. At the largest problem size, it reaches 0.2%–47% of the FPGA-optimized version's speed, a gap of 2.2× to 510×. By benchmark: CFD64 47%, CFD32 21%, FDTD2D 19%, SRAD 19%, NW 5.7%, LavaMD 4.0%, Where 3.0%, Raytracing 2.5%, ParticleFilter 0.3–0.4%, Mandelbrot 0.2%, k-Means 0.2% [self-reported timings; ratios derived]. Weckert, C., Solis-Vasquez, L., Oppermann, J., Koch, A., Sinnen, O., "Altis-SYCL: Migrating Altis Benchmarking Suite from CUDA to SYCL for GPUs and FPGAs," SC '23 Workshops, doi:10.1145/3624062.3624542; https://github.com/esa-tu-darmstadt/altis_sycl (`benchmarks.md`, commit b6b156a, Dec 2023).
- In the same data, the unchanged-source FPGA run is 63× (LavaMD) to 8,014× (ParticleFilter float) slower than the fastest CPU/GPU at the largest size. Even the optimized FPGA version is slower in all 12 cases, by 2.5× to 59×; it wins only at small sizes (k-Means 0.40×, LavaMD 0.44×, Where 0.61× the fastest CPU/GPU time). Treating every CPU/GPU efficiency as 1.0, which overstates them, six-platform PP is still only about 0.012 (k-Means, Mandelbrot) to 0.84 (CFD64) [derived]. Same source.
- How "same" the Altis source is: FDTD2D, Mandelbrot and NW have no FPGA-specific code, and LavaMD only has power-measurement macros. The other benchmarks add small `#ifdef _FPGA` blocks (work-group-size attributes, address space, a oneDPL FPGA policy). The optimized FPGA tree "can no longer be executed on CPU or GPUs" and uses pipes, replicated compute units and FPGA-only loop attributes (e.g. 26 `initiation_interval` uses). A whitespace-insensitive diff, which includes host code and re-wrapped lines, removes 26% (Raytracing) to 82% (Mandelbrot) of the non-blank lines, although the README calls these "minor code changes". Moving from Stratix 10 to Agilex needed further edits, and DWT2D has no optimized FPGA version [self-reported; diff derived]. Same source.
- The HPC Challenge (HPCC) FPGA suite keeps one OpenCL kernel file for both Intel and Xilinx FPGAs but branches inside it with `#ifdef INTEL_FPGA` (e.g. a GEMM shift-register buffer only on Intel). The README says that for Intel, "the device code has to be modified" for High Bandwidth Memory (HBM). Meyer, M., Kenter, T., Plessl, C., H2RC 2020, doi:10.1109/H2RC51942.2020.00007, and JPDC 160:79–89, 2022, doi:10.1016/j.jpdc.2021.10.007; https://github.com/pc2/HPCC_FPGA (`STREAM/src/device/stream_kernels_single.cl`, `GEMM/src/device/gemm_base.cl`).
- The oneAPI FPGA Optimization Guide (intel.com) was unreachable, so none of its statements are quoted. Intel's own FPGA samples at tag 2024.2.0 contain 231 `single_task` launches in 122 of 237 .cpp/.hpp files, against 7 `parallel_for` in a single file (board_test), and link to the Guide's "single-work-item kernel design guidelines" [derived]. The current README says FPGA support "was removed from the Intel oneAPI Toolkits starting 2025.1" and moved to Altera's tools. https://github.com/oneapi-src/oneAPI-samples (tag 2024.2.0, `DirectProgramming/C++SYCL_FPGA`; main-branch README).

**Halide (the counter-position)**
- Mainline Halide lists CPU targets (x86, ARM, Hexagon, PowerPC, RISC-V, WebAssembly) and GPU APIs (CUDA, OpenCL, Metal, DirectX 12, Vulkan), with no FPGA or microcontroller (MCU) target. Its local Laplacian generator has one algorithm and separate GPU and CPU schedules, commented as 2.9 ms on an RTX 2060 and 21.4 ms on an i9-9960X with 32 threads [self-reported]. The PLDI 2013 paper itself was not reached. Ragan-Kelley, J. et al., "Halide: A Language and Compiler for Optimizing Parallelism, Locality, and Recomputation in Image Processing Pipelines," PLDI 2013, doi:10.1145/2491956.2462176; https://github.com/halide/Halide (README; `apps/local_laplacian/local_laplacian_generator.cpp`).
- In the Halide-HLS fork, 9 example apps build for CPU, GPU and FPGA (HLS, high-level synthesis). Five of them (conv, feature2d, gaussian, harris, unsharp) keep one algorithm with three schedules. The other four (bilateral_grid, camera_pipe, camera_unsharp, stereo) use a separate `MyPipelineOpt` algorithm only for the FPGA, for example re-tiled "shuffled" input in bilateral_grid and changed index and type arithmetic in stereo and camera_pipe. No performance numbers were verified; the paper (arXiv:1610.09405) was unreachable. Pu, J. et al., https://github.com/jingpu/Halide-HLS (`apps/hls_examples/*/pipeline.cpp`).
- HeteroCL separates the algorithm from hardware customizations and reports 7.4× to 83× speedups on an AWS F1 FPGA, but against single-core, single-thread CPU runs, so these are not PP figures [self-reported]. Lai, Y.-H. et al., "HeteroCL: A Multi-Paradigm Programming Infrastructure for Software-Defined Reconfigurable Computing," FPGA 2019; https://github.com/cornell-zhang/heterocl

**MCU targets**
- None of the verified sources includes a microcontroller target.

**What this establishes**
- No verified study shows one unchanged source with acceptable performance across CPU, GPU and FPGA. The only one found that runs one source on all three (Altis-SYCL) puts the FPGA at 0.2–47% of its FPGA-optimized version and 63–8,014× behind the best CPU/GPU, and even the optimized FPGA code loses at the largest sizes (Weckert et al. 2023).
- Across CPUs and GPUs, single-source models can do well: Kokkos at 0.98–1.0 of CUDA on BabelStream, and OpenMP with PP 0.90 over 15 platforms. But PP falls to 0 as soon as one platform is unsupported, and compute-heavy codes reach only 0.46–0.85 of CUDA (Deakin et al. 2019, 2020; Pennycook et al. 2016 via the P3 library).
- On FPGAs, getting good performance means restructuring, not tuning. The fastest Rodinia FPGA kernels are rewrites of the GPU kernels, mostly into single-work-item form; Altis's optimized FPGA tree is a separate source that no longer runs on CPU/GPU; and Intel's samples are almost entirely `single_task` (Zohouri et al. 2016; Weckert et al. 2023; oneAPI-samples).
- What does carry over to FPGAs is "same semantics, different source": separate per-target trees, `#ifdef` branches even between FPGA vendors, and in Halide-HLS a separate algorithm definition for 4 of 9 apps. Code divergence is the metric for this, separate from PP (Harrell et al. 2018; Meyer et al. 2020/2022; Halide-HLS).
- Unverified here: Marowka's critiques, the PP values printed in the Pennycook papers, the SC16 speedup figures, the Optimization Guide's wording on GPU-style kernels, and Halide's PLDI numbers. Getting these needs either access to the publisher sites or a higher search budget.

The ratios above were derived with `../scripts/altis.py` (Altis-SYCL `benchmarks.md`), `pp.py` (performance portability from a results table) and `vscuda.py` (ratios against CUDA).
