# Working note 10: energy per operation across fabrics

Written 24 October 2026 by a research agent with web search; github.com was reachable and most other hosts were not, so many sources were confirmed from search extracts, as each bullet says. Not independently re-verified claim by claim, unlike notes 7 and 8.

**Access note.** Direct fetches to arxiv.org, ieeexplore, top500.org, eembc.org and the university hosts failed (DNS errors or proxy 403). Items marked "(extract)" are confirmed only from search-engine extracts of the source. Items marked "(GitHub, fetched)" were read directly. The web-search budget ran out before I could check the author lists for three papers; those are flagged where they appear.

**Energy per operation and per data movement**
- Horowitz's 45 nm, 0.9 V table, as reproduced by citing sources: 8b add 0.03 pJ, 32b add 0.1 pJ, 8b mult 0.2 pJ, 32b int mult 3.1 pJ, 16/32b FP add 0.4/0.9 pJ, 16/32b FP mult 1.1/3.7 pJ, 64-bit access to an 8 KB SRAM 10 pJ. The often-quoted "32b DRAM read 640 pJ" comes from these reproductions; I could not confirm the original DRAM row (1.3–2.6 nJ) or the 32 KB and 1 MB cache rows. [secondhand] — M. Horowitz, "1.1 Computing's energy problem (and what we can do about it)," ISSCC Digest of Technical Papers, 2014, pp. 10–14, DOI 10.1109/ISSCC.2014.6757323; reproduced in mlsysbook.ai/vol1/backmatter/appendix_assumptions.html and people.cs.nycu.edu.tw/~ttyeh/course/2025_Fall/IOC5009/slide/lecture-3.pdf
- The paper's text says a DRAM access costs "1 to 2nJ", "a couple of orders-of-magnitude higher than the cost of an internal cache access or functional operation (10pJ)". [secondhand: Scribd transcription] — same paper; scribd.com/document/776848597/ISSCC-2014-Horowitz
- Keckler et al. Table 1, for 40 nm: double-precision fused multiply-add (DFMA) about 50 pJ; 64-bit read from an 8 KB SRAM 14 pJ; wire energy 240 fJ/bit/mm. Their 10 nm projections (high-voltage/low-voltage) are DFMA 8.7/6.5 pJ, SRAM read 2.4/1.8 pJ, wire 150/115 fJ/bit/mm. The paper argues communication dominates energy and sets an exascale target of about 20 pJ per flop, memory included, at 20 MW (extract of a hosted PDF). — S. W. Keckler, W. J. Dally, B. Khailany, M. Garland, D. Glasco, "GPUs and the Future of Parallel Computing," IEEE Micro 31(5):7–17, 2011, DOI 10.1109/MM.2011.89
- Dally gives a 32-bit add as 20 fJ and moving its two 32-bit operands 1 mm as 1.9 pJ. Going off-chip is 320 pJ, and fetching two 32-bit words from main memory is 1.3 nJ, "64,000x" the add (extract; process node not given in the extract). — W. J. Dally, "On the model of computation: point," Communications of the ACM 65(9), 2022, DOI 10.1145/3548783

**Idle power and energy proportionality**
- From CPU utilization of more than 5,000 Google servers over six months, servers run mostly between 10 and 50 percent of maximum utilization. "Even an energy-efficient server still consumes about half its full power when doing virtually no work", so efficiency at low load is under half of peak (extract of hosted copies). — L. A. Barroso, U. Hölzle, "The Case for Energy-Proportional Computing," IEEE Computer 40(12):33–37, 2007, DOI 10.1109/MC.2007.443
- Component dynamic ranges attributed to that paper: server CPUs draw under one-third of peak power at very low load (dynamic range above 70%), while DRAM is under 50%, disk 25% and network switches 15%. [secondhand] — A. Paya, D. C. Marinescu (author names not verified), "Energy-aware Load Balancing Policies for the Cloud Ecosystem," arXiv:1401.2198, 2014

**FPGA versus ASIC**
- Comparing a 90 nm FPGA with 90 nm standard-cell ASICs: the area ratio averages 35 for circuits built only from look-up tables (LUTs) and flip-flops, falling to as little as 18 with hard multipliers and memories. Critical-path delay is roughly 3 to 4 times, and dynamic power is about 14 times. The 2006 conference version reported 40x area and about 12x dynamic power (extract; I did not verify whether power was metered or tool-estimated). — I. Kuon, J. Rose, "Measuring the Gap Between FPGAs and ASICs," IEEE TCAD 26(2):203–215, 2007, DOI 10.1109/TCAD.2006.884574

**Microcontroller energy benchmarks**
- ULPMark-CoreMark (ULPMark-CM) counts CoreMark iterations per millijoule. One EEMBC page adds "divided by 1000", so the two pages disagree on scale. Each entry needs one score at 3.0 V plus one at a "best" voltage, scores carry a ±3% tolerance, and measurements use the ST PowerShield (extract). — EEMBC, "ULPMark-CM," eembc.org/ulpmark/ulp-cm/; EEMBC press release, 22 Feb 2019, eembc.org/press/pressrelease/190222.html
- ULPMark-CoreProfile is 1000 divided by average power in µW over 50 iterations of a one-second duty cycle. The active part is about 3% of that cycle, so the score mainly reflects deep sleep and wake transitions (extract). — EEMBC, "ULPMark-CP," eembc.org/ulpmark/ulp-cp/
- ST lists the STM32U5 at 464 ULPMark-CP, 125 ULPMark-PP and 54 ULPMark-CM. Read as iterations per mJ, 54 means about 18.5 µJ per CoreMark iteration (my arithmetic). [self-reported] — STMicroelectronics, "STM32U5 series product overview" (undated), lt.farnell.com/.../microcontrollers-stm32u5-series-product-overview.pdf
- I found no official EEMBC energy metric for CoreMark-PRO; the standardized energy variant covers CoreMark only. One open project measured a full CoreMark run from flash at 1.368 mJ with a Joulescope. [self-reported] — OpenEM, "EM•Mark," docs.openem.org/advancing/emmark/
- EEMBC's EnergyRunner harness (GitHub, fetched) supports the ST LPM01A, Joulescope JS110 and Keysight N6705 meters. "Only ONE power supply is allowed to supply the DUT [device under test] during measurement"; the level-shifter supply is outside the boundary, and any other on-board circuitry counts against the score unless it is cut away. — EEMBC, energyrunner README, github.com/eembc/energyrunner (accessed 2026-10-24)

**Green500 (GFLOPS per watt)**
- June 2026 list: KAIROS (BullSequana XH3000, NVIDIA GH200, CALMIP/Toulouse) is first at 73.28 GFLOPS/W on 3.046 PFLOP/s of HPL (the LINPACK benchmark). ROMEO-2025 is second at 70.91, the Levante GPU extension third at 69.43, and Portage (AMD MI300A) tenth at 66.46. 73.28 GFLOPS/W works out to about 13.6 pJ per FP64 HPL flop at the system boundary (my arithmetic; extract). — TOP500, "Green500 June 2026," top500.org/lists/green500/green500-june-2026/
- Power measurement has three quality levels (EEHPC methodology, 2014 rules). Level 1 measures at least 1/64 of the machine or 1 kW over 20% of the run; Level 2 at least 1/8 or 10 kW over the full run; Level 3 the whole machine with continuously integrated energy. In November 2014, 233 of 267 submissions used derived estimates rather than measurements; current rules may differ (extract). — Green500 SC14 BoF, "Green500 Methodologies," 20 Nov 2014, top500.org/files/green500/141120-BOF-Green500-Methodologies.pdf

**WebAssembly versus native**
- On three microcontrollers (Raspberry Pi Pico, ESP32-C6, nRF5340), native C used 0.06–0.1 mJ per benchmark. On the Pico, bubble sort took 3.11 mJ under the wasm3 interpreter and 2.96 mJ under WAMR, about 50x native. Energy was computed as average active power times execution time, with interpreters only, no ahead-of-time or JIT compilation (extract; WAMR figure [secondhand] via aiotwin.eu). — Authors not verified, "WebAssembly on Resource-Constrained IoT Devices: Performance, Efficiency, and Portability," arXiv:2512.00035, 2025
- C, Rust, Go and JavaScript were compiled to Wasm on a Raspberry Pi 3B under Wasmer and Wasmtime. Source language mattered (up to 5.4x worse) and runtime choice was inconclusive. The replication package (GitHub, fetched) shows energy came from PowerJoular, a software estimator, and describes no native baseline. — L. Wagner, M. Mayer, A. Marino, A. Soldani Nezhad, H. Zwaan, I. Malavolta, EASE 2023, DOI 10.1145/3593434.3593454; github.com/S2-group/ease-2023-wasm-iot-rep-pkg
- Wasm against JavaScript (not native): about 30% average energy gain across Chrome, Edge and Firefox, but micro-benchmarks used 5.18% more energy under Wasm. A Monsoon power-monitor study on Android phones found Wasm 20–30% lower (extracts). — J. De Macedo, R. Abreu, R. Pereira, J. Saraiva, "WebAssembly versus JavaScript: Energy and Runtime Performance," 2022 (venue not confirmed), repositorio.inesctec.pt; Pockstaller, Huber, Demetz, WEBIST 2023, scitepress.org/Papers/2023/122056/122056.pdf; van Hasselt et al., EASE 2022, DOI 10.1145/3530019.3530034
- Performance only, no energy: on SPEC CPU, Wasm was 45% slower than native in Firefox and 55% slower in Chrome on average, with peaks of 2.08x and 2.5x. — A. Jangda, B. Powers, E. Berger, A. Guha, "Not So Fast: Analyzing the Performance of WebAssembly vs. Native Code," USENIX ATC 2019, arXiv:1901.09056

**Cross-fabric comparisons of the same kernel**
- On vision kernels, an Arm Cortex-A57 CPU, a Jetson TX2 GPU and a ZCU102 FPGA were compared. The GPU used 1.1–3.2x less energy per frame on simple kernels; the FPGA used 1.2–22.3x less on complex kernels and full pipelines. Each board was read from its own on-board power chips through a different path, and only dynamic power was counted, static excluded. There is no microcontroller (extract). — M. Qasaimeh, K. Denolf, J. Lo, K. Vissers, J. Zambreno, P. H. Jones, ICESS 2019, arXiv:1906.11879
- On sliding-window applications, FPGAs were most energy-efficient "in almost all situations". Energy was estimated as runtime times each device's worst-case power, not metered (extract). — J. Fowers, G. Brown, P. Cooke, G. Stitt, FPGA '12, DOI 10.1145/2145694.2145704
- On 15 Rodinia kernels with a power meter, the FPGA drew on average about 28% of the GPU's power and matched or beat it on 6 kernels (extract; authors not verified). — "Understanding Performance Differences of FPGAs and GPUs," FCCM 2018, sfu.ca/~zhenman/files/SC2-FCCM2018-GPU-FPGA.pdf
- MobileNetV2 and ResNet-50 were measured at the DC input on KV260 (FPGA), Coral Mini (ASIC), ROCK 3A and Jetson Nano (GPU). The KV260 at Int8 used about 5x less energy than the Jetson Nano at FP16, and standby power was the same order as or larger than inference power. There is no microcontroller (abstract extract; authors other than "Ning" not verified). — Z. Ning et al., "Power Consumption Benchmark for Embedded AI Inference," IADIS, 2024
- Four Arm boards (Raspberry Pi 4, Jetson Nano, Coral, Tinker Edge R) plus an Arduino Nano 33 BLE microcontroller were measured on a HM310P bench supply with a built-in meter; the Arduino idled at 0.036 W. There is no FPGA, and I could not confirm the Arduino was measured on the same supply (extract). — S. P. Baller et al., "DeepEdgeBench," IC2E 2021, arXiv:2108.09457
- MLPerf Tiny v0.7 put microcontrollers and an FPGA through the same EEMBC "ML Energy 1.0.1" harness (GitHub logs, fetched). The Pynq-Z2 FPGA (Zynq XC7Z020, open division, 3-bit keyword-spotting model KWS-W3A3) had a median of 30.667 µJ per inference at about 1.75 W and 57,257 inferences/s. ST's closed-division keyword-spotting entries used the reference model: NUCLEO-U575ZI-Q 1,482 µJ per inference at 27.2 mW, NUCLEO-H7A3ZI-Q 3,713 µJ, NUCLEO-L4R5ZI 4,636 µJ. These are different models in different divisions, with no CPU or GPU; the FPGA team used a Joulescope JS110 and an E3610A supply. [self-reported] — MLCommons, tiny_results_v0.7, github.com/mlcommons/tiny_results_v0.7 (2022); hls4ml and FINN teams, "Open-source FPGA-ML codesign for the MLPerf Tiny Benchmark," arXiv:2206.11791, 2022
- An iCE40UP5K FPGA ran a CNN at 819 µJ per inference (8.55 mW, 95.5 ms), more than 4x lower than an nRF52840 microcontroller running CMSIS-NN. The measurement method was not confirmed (extract; authors not verified). — "At the Edge of the Heart: ULP FPGA-Based CNN for On-Device Cardiac Feature Extraction…," arXiv:2604.25799, 2026

**The single-boundary study**
- I found no study that measures the same kernel in joules on a microcontroller, a CPU, a GPU and an FPGA under one measurement boundary. The closest cover three fabrics with one boundary per board (Qasaimeh: CPU, GPU, FPGA on separate on-board rails), one DC-input boundary without a microcontroller or discrete CPU (Ning), or microcontroller plus FPGA in one harness but on different models (MLPerf Tiny v0.7). The search budget ran out before every phrasing was tried, so this is "not found", not proof that none exists.

**What this establishes**
- The per-operation numbers are circuit-level estimates and projections at specific process nodes, not metered joules for a kernel. All three sources agree that moving data costs far more than arithmetic: about 0.03–3.7 pJ per operation against 10 pJ for SRAM and roughly 1–2 nJ for DRAM (Horowitz), 14 pJ SRAM against 50 pJ DFMA plus 240 fJ/bit/mm (Keckler), and 20 fJ against 1.3 nJ (Dally).
- Where the boundary is drawn, and how much idle or static power falls inside it, can decide the result. Servers draw about half of full power at idle (Barroso and Hölzle); standby is the same order as inference power on embedded boards (Ning); Qasaimeh excludes static power; EnergyRunner meters only the DUT supply.
- The published fabric ratios are partial and not comparable with each other. FPGA against ASIC is 35x area, 3–4x delay and 14x dynamic power at 90 nm (Kuon and Rose). CPU/GPU/FPGA energy ratios come from on-board rails (Qasaimeh), worst-case-power estimates (Fowers), a power meter without a microcontroller (FCCM 2018) or DC input (Ning).
- Standardized energy metrics exist only within a class and do not connect across classes: ULPMark and MLPerf Tiny for microcontrollers (EEMBC, MLCommons), and Green500 HPL efficiency for whole systems at about 13.6 pJ per FP64 flop with tiered measurement rules (TOP500, EEHPC). MLPerf Tiny comes closest to bridging microcontroller and FPGA, but with different models.
- Measured Wasm-against-native energy data is thin. Wasm interpreters on microcontrollers used about 50x native energy (arXiv:2512.00035). The EASE 2023 study used software estimation and no native baseline (Wagner et al.). Other energy results compare Wasm with JavaScript, about 20–30% lower (Macedo et al., Pockstaller et al.), while the native gap is documented mainly for speed, 45–55% (Jangda et al.).
