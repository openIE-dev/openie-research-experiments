# Continuous improvement without versions: what the software and hardware records say about the versioning problem, and where the RCE design stands

## Summary

The dependency and version problem of software is not a tooling accident but a consequence of three design choices that the record has examined separately: identity by mutable name plus asserted number, compatibility as a declared promise rather than a checked relation, and resolution of a global constraint problem at every consumer. Dependency resolution with one version per package and arbitrary constraints is NP-complete (Mancinelli et al. 2006; Abate et al. 2008, 2020; Cox 2016); semantic versioning is adopted but only partly honoured and cannot carry the information it promises (Raemaekers et al. 2017; Ochoa et al. 2022; Decan and Mens 2021; Gruevski 2023; Hyrum's Law); exposure is a property of the transitive graph (Decan, Mens and Grosjean 2019; Zimmermann et al. 2019; Wetter and Ringland 2021); and open ranges transmit breakage while restrictive ones block compatible upgrades in more than 16% of cases (Zerouali et al. 2018, 2019). Where the record converges is on five points: identity derived from content (Dolstra 2006; Unison; Software Heritage; Dhall), compatibility as a relation between two artifacts checked by a tool (Avro; Protocol Buffers; Elm; Rust RFC 1105 with cargo-semver-checks; Liskov and Wing 1994), additive change under names that are never recycled (Hickey 2016; Protocol Buffers `reserved`; Stripe 2017), coexistence instead of flag-day replacement (Erlang; Stripe; the one-version monorepo with atomic migration; commuting patches), and a direct proof that a change preserved behaviour (Godlin and Strichman 2009; Lahiri et al. 2012). The hardware record adds what software practice lacks: a formal equivalence check that gates every revision (Conformal, Formality; Baumgartner et al. 2006), audited baselines with as-built audits and problem-report-driven change control (ISO 10007; EIA-649; DO-254; ISO 26262-8), and an explicit cost of a failed change (mask sets beyond $10M at 7 nm; 86% of 2024 projects needing a respin). Hardware in turn lacks semantic diff on its canonical artifacts and deterministic rebuilds.

Read against that record, the RCE design already takes every one of the five converged positions as a rule of the specification: one language that is the text at one commit (2.7, C.15), definition hashes with a closed compatibility table (F.4.3), a manifest that pins by hash with no resolver and treats a published definition as immutable (F.5.3), name-free log hashes and replay (T7, T3), and behaviour equivalence as the only sense of "same" (6.10). What remains is listed in section 6: a whole-program hash, contracts and steps inside the definition hash, the equivalence check run as a gate on every change (hardware's equivalence check, in RCE's terms a bisimulation check with the examples as regression vectors), a program identity in the log, a version tag on the hash choice, the engine profile's residual MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH mechanism, and the stale per-version wording in three places of the text. The survey recommends no version selection, no ranges, no resolver and no registry; the mechanism that replaces continuous versioning is continuous admission at one commit, which is the hardware baseline-and-audit model with the audit automated.

## 2. The problem as the software record states it

**Resolution is hard by construction.** Installability in a Debian-style repository is NP-complete by reduction to and from SAT (Mancinelli et al. 2006; Abate et al. 2008), and the result holds "in all non-trivial component models" (Abate, Di Cosmo, Gousios and Zacchiroli 2020). Cox (2016) proved the same for language package managers by reduction from 3-SAT and noted the two escapes: let several versions coexist, or allow only minimum-version constraints. Practice follows those escapes or embeds a solver: Debian QA and opam run SAT and CUDF solvers; npm nests duplicate versions; Go's minimal version selection is polynomial because each module lists only minimums and a major version is a distinct import path (Cox 2018); Google removes the choice with the one-version rule (Potvin and Levenberg 2016; Google Open Source, "The One Version Rule").

**The number does not carry the promise.** About one third of Maven Central releases historically introduced a breaking change, in minor and patch releases as often as in major ones (Raemaekers, van Deursen and Visser 2017); a replication on 119,879 upgrades found 20.1% of non-major releases breaking and 7.9% of clients affected (Ochoa et al. 2022). Even in Rust, where the API is typed, about one release in thirty of the top 1,000 crates violates semantic versioning (Gruevski 2023). Compliance norms differ by community and most developers do not know the other communities' norms (Bogart et al. 2016, 2021). Hyrum's Law states the limit: with enough users, every observable behaviour is depended on, whatever the contract says. Software Engineering at Google concludes that semantic versioning is "a lossy summary of the perceived risk in adopting any particular change" (Winters, Manshreck and Wright 2020, chapter 21).

**Exposure is transitive.** A growing share of packages is fragile through transitive dependencies (Decan, Mens and Grosjean 2019); a small number of maintainer accounts could reach the majority of npm (Zimmermann et al. 2019); for more than 80% of the 35,863 Maven artifacts affected by Log4Shell the vulnerable dependency was more than one level deep, for a majority five levels (Wetter and Ringland 2021). left-pad (2016), event-stream (2018), colors and faker (2022) and xz (2024) each propagated through dependents that never named the offending package.

**Both pinning and ranges cost.** Open ranges transmitted the event-stream and colors payloads within a "compatible" range; restrictive constraints are the main driver of technical lag (dependencies lag 7 to 9 months behind the latest release; Decan, Mens and Constantinou 2018) and block backward-compatible upgrades in more than 16% of cases (Decan and Mens 2021).

## 3. What the software record converges on

1. **Identity from content.** Nix stores a component under a hash of all its build inputs, so variants coexist and dependencies are complete by construction (Dolstra, de Jonge and Visser 2004; Dolstra 2006; Courtès and Wurmus 2015). Unison identifies every definition by a hash of its syntax tree with names as metadata, and reports that dependency conflicts "are not an issue" because two versions are two values that coexist. Software Heritage identifiers are intrinsic: anyone can recompute them (Di Cosmo, Gruenpeter and Zacchiroli 2020). Dhall hashes the normal form of an expression, so a refactor keeps the hash and a behavioural change breaks it. Git itself is a content-addressable store (Chacon and Straub 2014). Lockfiles pin a version for people and record a content hash for the tool; the hash is what is checked.

2. **Compatibility as a checked relation between two artifacts.** Avro resolves a writer schema against a reader schema; Protocol Buffers and Thrift make the wire self-describing by numeric tag; the Java Language Specification defines binary compatibility relative to pre-existing binaries; Liskov and Wing (1994) define the substitution criterion. Elm derives the version bump from the typed API diff instead of trusting the author; cargo-semver-checks lints the API diff against RFC 1105 and can generate a witness program that compiles against the old version and fails against the new.

3. **Additive change under names that are never recycled.** Hickey (2016) classifies every change as growth (provide more, require less) or breakage (require more, provide less, or reuse a name for something else) and argues that breakage should become accretion under new names. Protocol Buffers forbid reusing a field number. Stripe keeps every API version since 2011 alive by transformation modules so the core has one shape (Leach 2017). GraphQL evolves without versions because clients name the fields they read.

4. **Coexistence instead of replacement.** Erlang keeps the old and the current instance of a module loaded; Google's monorepo forces atomic migration under one version; CRDTs converge without coordination (Shapiro et al. 2011; Kleppmann and Beresford 2017); patch theories make independent patches commute (Mimram and Di Giusto 2013; Pijul).

5. **A direct proof that a change preserved behaviour.** Regression verification proves equivalence between successive versions, which is often easier than full verification (Godlin and Strichman 2009); SymDiff checks equivalence of two imperative programs (Lahiri et al. 2012). Reproducible builds and signed provenance then make it verifiable which artifact a consumer runs (Lamb and Zacchiroli 2022; SLSA; in-toto; Sigstore).

## 4. What the hardware record adds

Hardware design has lived with an irreversible cost per failed change and built its process around it. A mask set costs beyond $10M at 7 nm (analyst estimate), first-silicon success fell to 14% of projects in 2024 (Siemens EDA and Wilson Research Group 2024), and a metal-only engineering change order rewires spare cells placed in advance so that only the metal masks are remade (Tan and Jiang 2011). Three practices follow that software does not have in mainstream form.

- **Equivalence checking as the gate on every revision.** Combinational equivalence checking proves the netlist equal to the RTL without test vectors at every step of the flow (Cadence Conformal; Synopsys Formality), and sequential equivalence checking proves it across retiming, clock gating and changed initialisation (Baumgartner et al. 2006). A functional ECO is verified on the affected logic cones only. The question "did this change preserve behaviour" is answered by proof, not by a version number.
- **Audited baselines and problem-report-driven change.** ISO 10007 (planning, identification, baselines, change control, status accounting, audit), SAE EIA-649C, MIL-HDBK-61A, DO-254 (a configuration item "modif[ied] in a controlled fashion"; HC1 items change only through a formal problem report), DO-178C Table A-8 and ISO 26262-8 clauses 7 and 8 all separate the baseline from the change and audit the as-built item against its documentation (the physical configuration audit). Drawing revisions are letters logged in a revision block (ASME Y14.35), not a promise about compatibility.
- **Continuous improvement as a process with a check step.** Deming's plan-do-check-act cycle, kaizen's pairing of improvement with maintenance of standards (Imai 1986) and Toyota's set-based concurrent engineering, which keeps alternatives open until the evidence decides (Ward et al. 1995; Sobek, Ward and Liker 1999), treat change as continuous and checked rather than as discrete releases.

Hardware's weaknesses are the mirror image. Its canonical artifacts diff only through rendered images or bespoke tools (KiCad, Altium), its builds are not deterministic (placement randomisation; no authoritative statement of bit-identical FPGA rebuilds was found), its mechanism for field updates, partial reconfiguration, "find[s] limited use in deployed systems" (Vipin and Fahmy 2018), analog blocks cannot be retargeted without manual re-qualification, and reuse without an explicit reuse design "often provided little or no benefit" (Keating and Bricaud 2002). The hardware construction languages (Chisel, FIRRTL, CIRCT, SpinalHDL, Amaranth) are an explicit attempt to import software's single-source, compiler-driven model (Bachrach et al. 2012; Izraelevitz et al. 2017).

## 5. Where the RCE design stands against the record

Each converged position of section 3, and the hardware gate of section 4, is already a rule of the specification or a theorem (section numbers are the specification's; details and quotations in `notes/rce_mechanisms.md`).

| Position of the record | RCE rule or theorem | Status |
|---|---|---|
| One version of everything, atomic migration (Google; Meta) | One language, the text at one commit; every module in it; the release label "selects nothing, gates nothing, is not hashed" (2.7); a meaning change is a new release, never a silent edit (C.15); the whole ecosystem built and checked from one commit; conformance runs against `rce` built from the same commit, "so there is no commit to pin" | In force; decision 64 retired per-module gating |
| Identity from content (Nix; Unison; SWHID; Dhall) | Definition hash over the name-free canonical form with the hashes of the items it names; layout, comments and order not hashed (F.4.3); `rce hash`; "A published definition is immutable: a hash names one text forever. A correction is a new definition with a new hash." (F.5.3); log hashes over positional forms, invariant under renaming (6.1, T7, proved) | Implemented in part: `impl` blocks, `step`s and contracts are outside the hash; no whole-program hash |
| Compatibility as a checked relation (Avro; Elm; cargo-semver-checks; Liskov and Wing) | Closed diff table of `rce api-diff`: compatible (export added; body change under unchanged signature, contracts and measure; `requires` weakened, `ensures` strengthened) and breaking (the rest, including an added field or variant); T50 | Implemented except the contract rows; T50 stated, unproved |
| No resolver; pin by hash (lockfiles; Go's minimums; Nix) | Manifest of module and dependency hashes; W-PIN-1; "Imports are an unordered set; adding an export is always compatible. There is no resolver: the manifest is the whole answer." (F.5.3) | `rce manifest-check` implemented; SLSA provenance, build cache and advisory keys not |
| Never recycle a name or number (Protocol Buffers; Hickey) | A release label is never reused (C.15); a definition hash names one text forever; one global reserved list (decision 64) | In force; the stdlib migration renamed `Json` and `Url` once, without aliases |
| Proof that a change preserved behaviour (equivalence checking; regression verification) | Behaviour is the only sense of "same" (6.10); equivalence is bisimilarity (T58, proved); `shape_ctl` invariant under renaming (T61, tested); examples are typed event traces replayed at admission (6.9); replay reproduces every state hash and output (T3, proved) | The check exists as `rce dim` and the examples; it is not yet run as a gate on a change |
| Audited baseline and as-built audit (ISO 10007; DO-254; PCA) | The log D is the baseline; verify on open (HS-4); a trace is the log and conformance to 6.1 implies conformance to the trace (10.9); 102 conformance tests pin trace heads | In force |
| Withdrawal without mutation (advisories) | A signed advisory "never changes what a hash means, only whether a tool warns on it" (F.5.3) | Format specified; key distribution not implemented |

The one place where the repository still carries semantic versioning is Appendix D, the engine profile: `spec NAME VERSION` in MAJOR.MINOR.PATCH, "not yet checked", with W-HS-VER-2 requiring MAJOR on removal and MINOR on addition, and a RESOLVE entry that records "the version of δ at its node". That is the asserted-number model the record rejects, inside a design whose other mechanisms replace it.

## 7. Continuous improvement in one commit

The record's answer to "how does a design improve continuously without versions" is the hardware one with the audit automated: a baseline, a change, a check that the change preserved what it was meant to preserve, and a log. In RCE's terms the cycle is: a proposal names the definitions it changes by hash; the checker admits it or names the rule it breaks; `rce api-diff` classifies every changed definition; the behaviour check and the examples decide whether the change is an erratum (behaviour preserved) or a meaning change (a new release of the whole text, never a per-module choice); the merge to the one commit is the atomic migration; the conformance suite and the trace fixtures are the as-built audit; and the log D of every running program is the baseline that replay verifies. Nothing in that cycle selects a version, resolves a range or consults a registry, and nothing in the record surveyed requires one.
