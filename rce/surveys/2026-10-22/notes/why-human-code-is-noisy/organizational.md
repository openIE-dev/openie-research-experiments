## KQ1. Conway's law and its empirical tests: organization structure as a source of interface noise

### Takeaway
The mirroring hypothesis is supported by the two strongest tests available: at Microsoft, organizational metrics predicted Vista post-release failures better than any code metric (precision 86.2% / recall 84.0% vs. 78.6/79.9 for churn), and across five matched open-source/commercial product pairs the commercially (tightly-coupled org) built product had 2.3x–6.1x higher propagation cost in every pair. Both are single-context or small-N studies; neither is a controlled experiment.

### Cited Findings
- Conway (1968), "How Do Committees Invent?", Datamation: "organizations which design systems ... are constrained to produce designs which are copies of the communication structures of these organizations." The paper's mechanism is that each design-team boundary becomes an interface in the product, and that the interface is as good as the communication across the team boundary. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Conway](http://www.melconway.com/Home/Conways_Law.html)
- Nagappan, Murphy & Basili (ICSE 2008), Windows Vista: 3,404 binaries, >50 MLOC, several thousand engineers; failures measured over the first six months post-release; 50 random 2/3–1/3 train/test splits. Eight organizational metrics (Number of Engineers, Ex-Engineers, Edit Frequency, Depth of Master Ownership, Percentage of Org contributing, Org Code Ownership, Overall Org Ownership, Org Intersection Factor). Precision/recall for predicting failure-prone binaries: organizational structure 86.2% / 84.0%; code churn 78.6% / 79.9%; code complexity 79.3% / 66.0%; dependencies 74.4% / 69.9%; code coverage 83.8% / 54.4%; pre-release bugs 73.8% / 62.9%. Authors note single-system generalizability limits. **[verified]** — [Nagappan et al. 2008 PDF](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/basili/publications/proceedings/P125.pdf)
- MacCormack, Baldwin & Rusnak (Research Policy 2012, "Exploring the duality between product and organizational architectures"): five matched pairs (OS pair measured twice, so six comparisons) of same-function products, open-source (loosely coupled org) vs commercial (tightly coupled org). Modularity measured as propagation cost (mean share of files affected by a change to a random file) from static call graphs. Propagation cost open-source vs commercial: financial mgmt 7.74% vs 47.14%; word processing 8.25% vs 41.77%; spreadsheet 23.62% vs 54.31%; Linux 2.1.32 vs Solaris 7.18% vs 22.59%; Linux 2.6.8 vs XNU 7.21% vs 24.83%; database 11.30% vs 43.23%. Open source more modular in every pair; most differences p<0.001; chance of all five favoring open source by coincidence ~3%. Authors state the difference is "up to a factor of eight" but Table 4 values give a max of ~6.1x (the 8x figure may use a different measure). Limitation: five pairs, software only, Gnumeric anomaly attributed to concentrated development. **[verified]** — [MacCormack et al. 2012 (Harvard DASH)](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/34403525/maccormack,baldwin,rusnak_exploring-the-duality.pdf)
- The RCE three-body essay already cites MacCormack et al. 2012 as "product architectures mirror organizational structure in the predicted direction." **[verified]** — [Three-Body essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/three-body/)

### Inferences
- The Vista result is the strongest quantitative link between organization and defect location: who-touched-it and how-many-orgs-touched-it beat every intrinsic code metric. This is directly the RCE claim that failures sit at handoffs: the Organization Intersection Factor and Depth of Master Ownership metrics are proxies for the number of organizational boundaries a binary crosses.
- The MacCormack result implies that propagation cost (the cross-module blast radius of a change) is a function of the org that built the code, not of the problem domain, since matched pairs hold function constant. Interface noise is therefore partly exogenous to the technical problem.

### Gaps
- No large-scale replication of the Vista organizational-metrics study at another company was located within call budget (Nagappan's later work and Bird et al. "Does distributed development affect software quality?" exist but were not fetched).
- Causality direction (org shapes code vs. code shapes org) is not settled by either study; MacCormack et al. discuss but do not resolve it.

## KQ2. Lehman's laws, measured growth/complexity curves, entropy over commit histories, technical-debt economics

### Takeaway
Continuing change and continuing growth are confirmed in every empirical test; increasing complexity is confirmed in absolute terms but mixed when normalized; declining quality is contradicted (defect density falls). Technical debt is dominated by architectural choices (54% of practitioners rank bad architecture top-3; 73% of free-text examples), and the dollar figures in circulation (CISQ $2.41T, Stripe $85B) are model estimates, not accounted costs.

### Cited Findings
- Lehman (1980, Proc. IEEE, "Programs, Life Cycles, and Laws of Software Evolution"; extended 1996): Law I Continuing Change (an E-type system must be continually adapted or becomes progressively less satisfactory); Law II Increasing Complexity (as a system evolves its complexity increases unless work is done to maintain or reduce it); later laws on self-regulation, conservation of organizational stability and familiarity, continuing growth, declining quality, feedback system. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Lehman 1980 (IEEE)](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/1456074)
- Neamtiu, Xie & Chen (J. Softw. Maint. Evol. 2013, "Towards a better understanding of software evolution: an empirical study on open-source software"): nine C programs, 705 releases, ~108 cumulative years (Bash, BIND, Bison, OpenSSH, Quagga, Samba, Sendmail, SQLite, Vsftpd). Laws I (continuing change) and VI (continuing growth) confirmed for all. Law II: absolute cyclomatic complexity and absolute common coupling increase for all programs (p≈0), normalized cyclomatic complexity mostly increasing and significant; normalized coupling text/table inconsistent. Law VII (declining quality) contradicted: defect density falls. Laws III, IV, V, VIII not confirmed for all. Growth exponents (size ~ x^n): Bash 0.368, OpenSSH 0.432, Sendmail 0.559, SQLite 0.348, Vsftpd 0.367, BIND dev 0.251, Quagga 0.063 (weak fit); super-linear Bison 1.649, Samba dev 2.335; Samba 3.0.X maintenance 1.036. Maintenance branches at most linear. **[verified]** — [Neamtiu et al. PDF](https://www.cs.kent.edu/~jmaletic/cs63902/Papers/jsme11neamtiu.pdf)
- Torres, Baltes, Treude & Wagner (arXiv 2303.13729, Mar/Apr 2023, "Applying Information Theory to Software Evolution"): 25 open-source projects' commit histories; two entropy definitions (structural, textual) that are highly correlated with each other but show weak and unstable correlations with other complexity metrics; outlier analysis finds an unexpectedly high frequency of large information-content changes ("surprising changes"). Commit counts not stated in abstract; no bug or growth linkage reported there. **[verified]** — [arXiv 2303.13729](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.13729v2). Note: RCE §2.1 describes this paper as finding "the two rise together"; the abstract says "highly correlated," which is consistent but weaker than a monotone-rise claim. **[verified]** — [RCE paper](https://research.openie.dev/papers/rce/)
- Cunningham (OOPSLA 1992 experience report, WyCash): the debt metaphor — shipping first-time code is like going into debt; "a little debt speeds development so long as it is paid back promptly with a rewrite"; danger when debt is not repaid, "every minute spent on not-quite-right code counts as interest." **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Cunningham 1992](http://c2.com/doc/oopsla92.html)
- Ernst, Bellomo, Ozkaya, Nord & Gorton (ESEC/FSE 2015, "Measure it? Manage it? Ignore it?"): 1,831 respondents began the survey across three organizations (two multinationals, one government lab), 536 completed all questions, response rate 29%; seven interviews. Bad architecture choices ranked top-3 source of technical debt by 54% (296/544), mean rank 4.3, ahead of overly complex code (6.0) and lack of documentation (6.5); 73% of coded open-ended examples were architecture choices; 89% of respondents on systems ≥6 years old agreed architecture is a significant debt source vs 80% on systems <3 years. 65% had no defined TD management practice; 27% did not identify TD at all; 42% said business managers largely unaware; 10% said managers actively managed it; 41% used no tools, only 16% said tools gave appropriate detail; 75% agreed TD consequences had consumed "a painful chunk" of resources (n=574). Coding reliability Cohen's kappa 0.45 (low-moderate). **[verified]** — [Ernst et al. 2015 PDF](https://cs.unibg.it/esecfse_proceedings/fse15/p50-ernst.pdf)
- CISQ 2022 (as cited in RCE interface-economics essay): cost of poor software quality in the US at least $2.41T in 2022 plus ~$1.52T accumulated technical debt; built partly on other estimates; covers software quality generally, not interfaces. **[estimate; verified via RCE essay]** — [Interface economics essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/interface-economics/)
- Stripe/Harris Poll 2018 (as cited in the same essay): ~17.3 h/week per developer on maintenance, ~4 h/week on "bad code," valued at nearly $85B/year worldwide. **[estimate; verified via RCE essay]** — [Interface economics essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/interface-economics/)
- The RCE essay explicitly refuses to total these figures because "no primary source isolates the cost of interface failures," and frames technical debt as "a balance of unexamined interface assumptions" with interest = recurring cost of unchecked mismatches. **[verified]** — [Interface economics essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/interface-economics/)

### Inferences
- Measured growth in long-lived C projects is mostly sub-linear in release count, not exponential; the super-linear cases (Bison, Samba dev branch) are development branches, i.e., where new intent is admitted. Maintenance branches (frozen intent) grow at most linearly. This matches a model where growth is driven by the admission rate of new intent, not by time.
- The debt literature locates the bulk of debt in architecture (interface and decomposition decisions), which is where handoffs are defined; this is consistent with the RCE/interface-economics framing but the Ernst survey does not itself measure handoffs.

### Gaps
- Lehman's original 1996 "Laws of software evolution revisited" was not fetched; dates and law numbering from memory.
- Torres et al. full text not read; whether entropy rises monotonically over history is unverified beyond the abstract.
- No peer-reviewed source for CISQ or Stripe figures; both are vendor/industry estimates with undisclosed sampling.

## KQ3. Committee design and feature accretion in languages; what kept small languages small

### Takeaway
The pattern across Hoare (1980), Wirth (1995), Stroustrup (2018–2020), Go (2018–2025), Lua (2018) and Elm (2019–) is consistent: languages grow when the admission process is enthusiasm-weighted and open to many proposers; they stay small when there is a single or tiny decision locus, an explicit bias toward decline, a rule that new needs must be met by composing existing mechanisms, or an outright freeze. Measured: Go declines 40.9% of proposals and accepts 30.8%; WG21 has 200–250 attendees and >500 papers/year; Lua is ~25,000 lines of C and a ~100-page manual.

### Cited Findings
**Committee accretion (anecdotal / testimony):**
- Hoare, 1980 Turing Award lecture "The Emperor's Old Clothes": "There are two ways of constructing a software design: One way is to make it so simple that there are obviously no deficiencies, and the other way is to make it so complicated that there are no obvious deficiencies. The first method is far more difficult." "The price of reliability is the pursuit of the utmost simplicity. It is a price which the very rich find most hard to pay." On committees: "No committee will ever do this until it is too late." Hoare sat on the PL/I technical committee and advised Ada's designers; warns that a technically unsound project can still be "doomed to succeed" because money and determination push it through, but reliability cannot be bought that way. "Our main failure was overambition." **[verified via Acolyer summary]** — [The Morning Paper on Hoare 1980](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/09/07/the-emperors-old-clothes/)
- Wirth, "A Plea for Lean Software," IEEE Computer, Feb 1995: names "rapidly growing hardware performance" and "customers' ignorance of features that are essential versus nice-to-have" as causes of fat software; "these details are cute but not essential, and they have a hidden cost"; people mistake complexity for sophistication. Oberon (1986–89) was built to show software could run in far less memory/CPU without loss of function. Wirth attributes "software is getting slower more rapidly than hardware becomes faster" to Martin Reiser (Oberon System book preface, 1991: "The hope is that the progress in hardware will cure all software ills"). **[verified via Wikipedia summary; original at IEEE]** — [Wirth's law (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wirth%27s_law); [IEEE Xplore 348001](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/348001). Wirth's specific time-pressure and monolithic-design causes were not confirmed in the fetched summary; the Oberon KB figures in the original article were not retrieved.
- Stroustrup, "Remember the Vasa!" (WG21 paper P0977, 2018, reported by The Register June 2018): "Individually, many proposals make sense. Together they are insanity to the point of endangering the future of C++"; C++ "could crumble under the weight of these – mostly not quite fully-baked – proposals"; ~160 people at the Rapperswil meeting, ~70 organizations and 11 nations formally voting, 43 proposals cited in the letter, 400 committee papers that year by June. **[verified]** — [The Register 2018](https://www.theregister.com/2018/06/18/bjarne_stroustrup_c_plus_plus/)
- Stroustrup, HOPL-IV (2020) "Thriving in a Crowded and Changing World: C++ 2006–2020": founding meeting Dec 1989 had ~25 people; for C++17/C++20 "as many as 250 people turned up at each face-to-face WG21 meeting" from a membership about twice that; paper collection grows by "more than 500 papers a year"; document volume roughly doubled vs early 2010s; fall-2018 pre-meeting mailing ~3x the words of Shakespeare's complete works; "the feature set grows contiguously," nothing significant is removed; "Most committee members like to see improvement; that is, to see features added"; "It is easier to summon enthusiasm for something new than for opposing it"; C++ is shaped by three contradictory demands: simplify, add essential features now, don't break existing code. **[verified]** — [Stroustrup HOPL-IV PDF](https://www.stroustrup.com/hopl20main-p5-p-bfc9cd4--final.pdf)
- Gabriel, "Lisp: Good News, Bad News, How to Win Big" (1991), "Worse is Better" section: the "New Jersey" style (simplicity of implementation over interface correctness/completeness) has better survival characteristics than the "MIT/right thing" style; "the right thing" takes too long and is too complex to spread; worse-is-better software "spreads like a virus" and then gets improved to 90% of right. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Gabriel, Worse is Better](https://www.dreamsongs.com/WorseIsBetter.html)
- Hickey, "Simple Made Easy" (Strange Loop 2011): simple = one fold/braid (objective, about interleaving), easy = near at hand (relative, about familiarity); teams choose easy over simple because the cost of complexity is deferred and the benefit of easy is immediate; "complecting" as the source of incidental complexity. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [InfoQ recording](https://www.infoq.com/presentations/Simple-Made-Easy/)

**Decline-biased governance (measured):**
- Go proposal process (Cox/Griesemer, Go blog "Go 2, here we come!", Nov 2018): ~120 open Go 2 proposal issues; 3 selected for Go 1.13; two rounds of community feedback that make the process "slanted towards declining proposals"; a proposal must "address an important issue for many people," "have minimal impact on everybody else," and "come with a clear and well-understood solution"; rule of thumb: help ≥10x as many developers as a change hurts. **[verified]** — [Go blog](https://go.dev/blog/go2-here-we-come)
- Empirical study of declined Go proposals (arXiv 2510.06984, Oct 2025): 1,095 proposals in golang/go, 785 with final decision; 448 declined (40.9%), 337 accepted (30.8%); median resolution 31 days declined / 37 days accepted; 14.7% of declined proposals resubmitted, of which 53.3% declined again and 25.3% accepted. Reasons for decline (coded on 180 proposals): poor feasibility 22.8%, deprecated 18.1%, limited use cases 13.2%, breaking Go's principles 11.4%, existing alternatives 10.3%, lack of knowledge 9.4%, duplication 8.7%, no consensus 5.8%, license 0.4%. Authors frame the high decline rate as careful review; they do not themselves claim it keeps the language small. **[verified]** — [arXiv 2510.06984](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06984v1)
- Lua (Ierusalimschy, de Figueiredo, Celes, CACM Nov 2018 "A Look at the Design of Lua"): implementation ~25,000 lines of C; 64-bit Linux binary ~200 KB; reference manual ~100 pages; runs in ~300 KB memory; motto "mechanisms instead of policies"; one general mechanism per major aspect (tables for data, functions for abstraction, coroutines for control); modules, objects, exceptions are built from these rather than added as features; special syntax avoided because syntax is unreachable from the C API while functions map to it; acknowledged cost: less convenience and "many incompatible object systems." **[verified]** — [Lua CACM 2018 PDF](https://www.lua.org/doc/cacm2018.pdf)
- Elm: stable release 0.19.1 dated 21 Oct 2019 with no later release listed as of this check; 0.19.0 removed "native" (kernel) code access for non-core packages; the compiler enforces semantic versioning on packages (removing a function or changing its type requires a major release). **[verified]** — [Elm (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elm_(programming_language))

### Inferences
- The accretion mechanism is an incentive asymmetry Stroustrup names outright: proposers are enthusiastic and concentrated, opponents are diffuse, and nothing is removed. A committee of 250 voting members with >500 papers/year is a proposal-generation machine with no symmetric removal process.
- The small-language cases share one of three structural rules: (a) a composition rule — needs must be met by combining existing mechanisms (Lua, and Go's "existing alternatives" + "breaking principles" decline reasons together ~22%); (b) a decline-default — the process is explicitly slanted to "no" and requires a 10:1 benefit/harm ratio (Go); (c) a freeze or single-maintainer veto (Elm, and effectively Lua's three-author core). None relies on reviewer taste alone; each is a rule about what counts as admissible.
- Gabriel's and Meyerovich's findings (KQ5) jointly explain why committee languages nevertheless win: adoption is driven by libraries and existing code, not by language quality, so bloat is not punished by the market on the timescale at which it is incurred.

### Gaps
- The "Glasgow 2014 study of language manual page counts" was not located. Searches on specification page-count growth returned only forum material; no peer-reviewed page-count study was found. Treat as unverified unless the RCE paper's own bibliography supplies it.
- Scala and Swift feature-growth critiques: not researched within budget (anecdotal blog literature exists; no measured study found).
- C++ standard page counts by edition were not retrieved from a primary source.
- Hoare's exact remarks on Algol 68 and PL/I feature-by-feature were not in the fetched summary.

## KQ4. Incentives: careerism, NIH, lock-in, open-source maintainer dynamics, leaky abstractions, duplicate ecosystems

### Takeaway
Evidence in this area is mostly testimonial. The one measured item is Stroustrup's attendance/paper-volume data (KQ3). Spolsky's leaky-abstraction argument and Eghbal's few-maintainer observation are frameworks, not measurements; no quantitative study of resume-driven complexity or NIH was found within budget.

### Cited Findings
- Spolsky, "The Law of Leaky Abstractions" (Nov 2002): "All non-trivial abstractions, to some degree, are leaky"; abstractions save time working but do not save time learning, because when the abstraction leaks the programmer must understand the layer beneath; each layer therefore adds learning cost without removing the underlying complexity. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Joel on Software](https://www.joelonsoftware.com/2002/11/11/the-law-of-leaky-abstractions/). The RCE three-body essay cites this under maker-to-user handoffs. **[verified]** — [Three-Body essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/three-body/)
- Eghbal, "The rise of few-maintainer projects" (Increment, 2019) and *Working in Public* (2020): modern open source is organized as casual contributions filtered by a very small core of maintainers; the maintainer's scarce resource is attention, not code; the Increment page fetched carried only the abstract, so no figures were retrieved. **[partially verified]** — [Increment](https://increment.com/open-source/)
- Stroustrup (HOPL-IV): committee members "like to see ... features added"; enthusiasm for new is easier than opposition; "perfectionism" and problem-imagination drive mission creep. **[verified]** — [Stroustrup HOPL-IV PDF](https://www.stroustrup.com/hopl20main-p5-p-bfc9cd4--final.pdf)
- Hoare 1980: a technically unsound project may be "doomed to succeed" when money and determination are committed — the political irreversibility of a large program. **[verified via summary]** — [Acolyer on Hoare](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/09/07/the-emperors-old-clothes/)
- Interface-economics essay: "an interface is a public good with private costs. Whoever writes and checks an assumption pays for everyone's benefit, so it is often underprovided" — the stated economic reason interfaces stay unexamined. **[verified]** — [Interface economics essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/interface-economics/)

### Inferences
- The public-good framing is the economic core: closing an interface (documenting units, ranges, versions) is paid privately and benefits everyone downstream, so under-provision is the equilibrium. Feature addition has the opposite sign: benefit is private and immediate (proposer, promotion, product), cost is diffuse and deferred (every implementer, teacher, reader). Both asymmetries push toward more surface and fewer checks.
- Maintainer scarcity in open source converts into a de facto decline bias in small projects (Lua, Elm) and into accretion in large ones where contributor volume exceeds review capacity (the ">500 papers/year" regime).

### Gaps
- No quantitative study of resume-driven development, promotion-driven architecture, or NIH was found. These remain anecdotal.
- Vendor lock-in / deliberate incompatibility: not researched within budget; standard references (Shapiro & Varian 1998 *Information Rules*; Farrell & Klemperer 2007 handbook chapter on switching costs) were not fetched.
- Platform-competition duplicate ecosystems (e.g., parallel GUI toolkits, parallel package managers): no measured study found.

## KQ5. Path dependence, network effects, and why worse languages win

### Takeaway
The economics literature splits: David (1985) argues standards can lock in inferior designs; Liebowitz & Margolis (1990) found "virtually no evidence" QWERTY is inferior. For languages, the one large empirical study (Meyerovich & Rabkin, OOPSLA 2013) finds adoption is driven by libraries and existing code, with intrinsic language features ranking far lower — which is the mechanism by which accretion goes unpunished.

### Cited Findings
- David, "Clio and the Economics of QWERTY" (AER 1985): QWERTY persisted via technical interrelatedness, scale economies and quasi-irreversibility of investment (typist training), illustrating lock-in to a possibly inferior standard. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [David 1985 (JSTOR)](https://www.jstor.org/stable/1805621)
- Liebowitz & Margolis, "The Fable of the Keys" (J. Law & Econ 1990): reject David's and Arthur's claim that QWERTY is inferior to Dvorak; using typing experiments, ergonomics studies and the competitive history of keyboard designs they find "virtually no evidence" for inferiority and conclude QWERTY is "about as good a design as any alternative." **[verified abstract]** — [SSRN](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=1069950)
- Meyerovich & Rabkin, "Empirical analysis of programming language adoption" (OOPSLA 2013): extrinsic factors (library availability, social factors) matter much more than intrinsic language features; languages grow "niche-by-niche" (NumPy for scientific Python, Rails for web); developers rank libraries first and performance second, yet language features that deliver performance rank much lower ("gap between the importance of performance and the language features used to achieve it today"); only ~1/3 of developers find static types valuable vs ~2/3 for unit testing; C++ by far the hardest language to master; number of languages known barely varies with age. **[verified via secondary summary; primary PDF and ACM page both failed to fetch]** — [Haldar summary](https://vivekhaldar.com/articles/empirical-analysis-of-programming-language-adoption); [ACM DOI 10.1145/2509136.2509515](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2509136.2509515)
- Meyerovich & Rabkin, "Socio-PLT" (Onward! 2012): many language features "provide benefits that programmers cannot directly and immediately observe," analogized to public-health observability problems. **[verified abstract]** — [Cornell TPLS listing](https://research.cs.cornell.edu/tpls/papers/20140414.shtml)
- Gabriel 1991: worse-is-better designs win because implementation simplicity lets them spread before the "right thing" is finished. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Worse is Better](https://www.dreamsongs.com/WorseIsBetter.html)

### Inferences
- If adoption is driven by existing code and libraries, the installed base is the selection pressure, not the language. A language can therefore accrete indefinitely without losing users, so long as it does not break existing code — which is exactly Stroustrup's third contradictory demand and Go's "minimal impact on everybody else" criterion. Backward compatibility is the mechanism that converts path dependence into accretion: you can add but not remove.
- The Liebowitz–Margolis critique matters for RCE: lock-in to an inferior standard is contested even in the canonical case, so "worse languages win" should be stated as "languages with more existing code win," which Meyerovich & Rabkin support directly.

### Gaps
- The exact dataset sizes of Meyerovich & Rabkin (SourceForge project count, Ohloh, survey N) were not retrieved; the primary PDF was unreachable. Secondary sources commonly cite ~200,000 SourceForge projects and survey responses in the thousands; treat as unverified.
- No study quantifying language switching costs in dollar or hour terms was found.

## KQ6. Handoff failures: specification vs implementation gaps, requirements volatility, information hiding, DevOps, interface economics

### Takeaway
The RCE essays already carry the strongest cited material here (Zave & Jackson, Naur, Mars Climate Orbiter, Ariane 5, Sculley et al. 2015, Newcombe et al. 2015). The organizational answer in the literature is Parnas (1972): decompose by what is likely to change and hide it, i.e., design the interface as a boundary of ignorance. Requirements-volatility figures were not retrieved within budget.

### Cited Findings
- RCE §2.1: Brooks's essence/accident split read as "the essence is the conceptual construct, and most of it is specification and design, not representation"; Zave & Jackson cited for the gap between specification and the unwritten domain knowledge that fills it; Naur cited for a program being the visible part of a theory not recoverable from the text; Lehman paraphrased on complexity growth "unless work reduces it." **[verified]** — [RCE paper](https://research.openie.dev/papers/rce/)
- Three-body essay: "the instability of computing systems is located at their unexamined interfaces, not in the bodies"; handoffs carry assumptions about units, ranges, timing, versions and intent; theorist→maker (Mars Climate Orbiter 1999, lbf·s vs N·s), maker→user (Hyrum's law, Spolsky; Ariane 5 1996, 64-bit to 16-bit conversion, breakup ~40 s), user→theorist is the least examined return path (Zave & Jackson; Hooker's hardware lottery); Sculley et al. 2015 — most ML maintenance cost is glue code and interfaces; Newcombe et al. 2015 — AWS TLA+ found design bugs review and testing missed; author's own caveat that the configuration is not shown to extend to multi-organization stacks. **[verified]** — [Three-Body essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/three-body/)
- Interface-economics essay accounted costs: Mars Surveyor 1998 project $327.6M ($193.1M spacecraft dev, $91.7M launch, $42.8M ops), orbiter alone ~$125M; ESA Cluster II recovery 214M ECU approved 1997, 1996 rebuild option priced 351M accounting units. Model estimates: NIST 2002 testing infrastructure $22.2B–$59.5B/yr; NIST 2004 interoperability in capital facilities $15.8B/yr. Propositions E1–E3 (cost-weighted share of failures at interfaces exceeds within-body share; a check pays when cost per crossing < P(failure) × summed downstream cost; machine-checked shared representations lower one party's cost of closing an interface). **[verified]** — [Interface economics essay](https://research.openie.dev/papers/interface-economics/)
- Parnas, "On the Criteria To Be Used in Decomposing Systems into Modules" (CACM 1972): decompose around design decisions likely to change and hide each inside a module; interfaces should reveal as little as possible; a decomposition by flowchart steps produces interfaces that leak data formats and force coordinated change. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Parnas 1972 (ACM)](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/361598.361623)
- Conway 1968 (KQ1) is itself a handoff theory: each inter-team boundary in the org becomes an interface in the product, and the quality of the interface equals the quality of communication across the boundary. **[canonical, not fetched]** — [Conway](http://www.melconway.com/Home/Conways_Law.html)
- Nagappan 2008 (KQ1): the Organization Intersection Factor and Depth of Master Ownership metrics measure how many org units touch a binary and how deep in the hierarchy ownership sits; these organizational handoff proxies outperformed all code metrics. **[verified]** — [Nagappan et al. 2008](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/basili/publications/proceedings/P125.pdf)

### Inferences
- Parnas and Conway together give the organizational prescription: choose module boundaries so that the thing most likely to change is owned by one party, so that change does not cross a handoff. The Vista data is the measurement of what happens when that is violated (ownership spread across org units predicts failure).
- DevOps / "you build it you run it" (Vogels, Amazon, 2006) is the same move applied to the maker→user handoff: collapse two bodies into one. No measured study of its defect effect was fetched; treat as practitioner claim.

### Gaps
- Requirements volatility figures (e.g., Standish/CHAOS, Jones) not retrieved; those sources are vendor/consultancy and would need flagging anyway.

## KQ7. What AI authorship changes: code volume, duplication, churn, and the model as a new "body"

### Takeaway
Vendor data (GitClear, 2024 and 2025 reports) shows duplicated code rising from 8.3% to 12.3% of changed lines (2021→2024), moved/refactored code falling from 25% to <10%, and churn rising from 3–4%/yr to 5.5% (2023) with a 7%+ projection for 2024; the one large academic study found (37,623 agent PRs, 2,807 repos, Dec 2024–Jul 2025) finds effects are vendor-specific, with revert rates ranging from 6.1% (Codex) to 14.5% (Devin) vs 11.5% human. The RCE interface-economics essay's hypothesis — generated code crosses the same interfaces with the same unchecked assumptions — is not yet tested by any source found.

### Cited Findings
- GitClear 2024 report (via Arc summary): 153M changed lines, 3,400 repositories, 2020–2023. Churn (code reverted or significantly altered within two weeks): 3–4%/yr in 2020–2022, 5.5% in 2023, projected >7% in 2024 (double the 2021 rate; projection, not observation). "Move" operations down 17% vs 2021; duplicated code share up 11% vs 2021 (relative changes; absolutes only in charts). Code replaced within two weeks up 10%; code older than one month changed 24% less often in 2023 than 2022. Caveats: duplication measured only within a single commit (true rate likely higher); Copilot prevalence (0% 2021, 5–10% 2022, 30% 2023) comes from GitHub/O'Reilly, not GitClear; the reported Pearson r=0.98 between Copilot use and churn is a correlation across 3–4 annual points; operation classification is an approximation of intent. **[vendor; verified via secondary summary]** — [Arc summary of GitClear 2024](https://arc.dev/developer-blog/impact-of-ai-on-code/)
- GitClear 2025 report (Jan 2025): 211M changed lines, Jan 2020–Dec 2024, repos owned by Google, Microsoft, Meta and enterprise C-corps (selection/weighting undisclosed). Cloned/copy-pasted code rose from 8.3% to 12.3% of changed lines (2021→2024); refactoring (moved code) fell from 25% of changed lines (2021) to <10% (2024); copy/paste exceeded moved code for the first time; headline "4x more code cloning" is not reconciled with the 8.3→12.3 figures (~48% relative rise), so the 4x likely refers to a different measure; "10% of all authored commits" containing cloned blocks is a projection; churn "increased" without a number on the page; how AI assistance was identified in commits is not described; full methodology in a gated PDF. - Academic: "Not All Agents Are Equal: Code Quality and Post-Merge Maintenance Across Five Autonomous Coding Agents in the Wild" (arXiv 2609.17598, 2026): OpenAI Codex, Devin, GitHub Copilot, Cursor, Claude Code vs matched human baseline; 37,623 provenance-labeled PRs from 2,807 GitHub repos, Dec 2024–Jul 2025 (AIDev dataset + 58,792 cached GitHub API responses). It may not admit them" — the check, not the model, decides what enters; §6: lift is "the door into the ecosystem for code written elsewhere" and "Inside the ecosystem these faults cannot be written"; §6.0: "New intent is what a supplier (a specification, a test, a person or a local model) provides once." **[verified]** — [RCE paper](https://research.openie.dev/papers/rce/)

### Inferences
- The GitClear signal, if it holds, is exactly Lehman Law II at higher gain: added lines up, moved (refactoring) lines down, so complexity-reducing work falls as a share of change. Duplication within a commit is the cheapest form of accidental complexity to produce and the kind a model produces when it lacks the "theory" (Naur) of the existing code.
- The 2026 agent study's vendor-specific results argue against treating "the model" as one body; each agent is a different maker with a different handoff profile (review latency, revert rate). The 12.6-hour median wait for first human review on one vendor's PRs is a measured handoff delay at the maker→reviewer boundary.
- Under RCE's own design (model proposes, check admits), the model is not a fourth body in the three-body sense; it is a supplier of intent bits into the theorist→maker handoff, and the check is what closes that interface. The GitClear duplication rise is what happens when the admission check is a human reviewer whose attention does not scale with generation rate.

## KQ8. Structural rules that kept languages small, and what they imply for RCE spec governance

### Takeaway
Across the cases above, smallness was never the result of good taste alone; it was the result of a rule that made addition expensive relative to composition. Five such rules recur: single decision locus, decline-default with an explicit benefit/harm ratio, composition-before-addition, no-removal-means-no-addition (or symmetric removal), and freezing the surface while letting the ecosystem move. For RCE the implication is that the spec's admission check should be mechanical (the check admits, not the committee), and that the governance structure should be designed to the Nagappan/MacCormack results: minimize the number of organizational units whose boundary runs through the spec.

### Cited Findings (consolidated from above)
- Decline-default with explicit ratio: Go requires ≥10:1 helped:hurt and two feedback rounds "slanted towards declining"; measured outcome 40.9% declined / 30.8% accepted over 1,095 proposals. **[verified]** — [Go blog](https://go.dev/blog/go2-here-we-come); [arXiv 2510.06984](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.06984v1)
- Composition-before-addition: Lua's "mechanisms instead of policies," one mechanism per aspect, new needs met by combining tables/functions/coroutines; cost: 25k lines of C, ~100-page manual, ~200 KB binary; acknowledged downside: many incompatible user-level object systems. **[verified]** — [Lua CACM 2018](https://www.lua.org/doc/cacm2018.pdf)
- Freeze: Elm 0.19.1 (Oct 2019) with no subsequent release; kernel-code access removed from non-core packages in 0.19.0; compiler-enforced semver. **[verified]** — [Elm (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elm_(programming_language))
- Accretion regime: WG21 ~250 attendees, >500 papers/yr, "the feature set grows contiguously," nothing removed, enthusiasm asymmetry. **[verified]** — [Stroustrup HOPL-IV](https://www.stroustrup.com/hopl20main-p5-p-bfc9cd4--final.pdf)
- Organizational boundary → interface defect: Nagappan 2008 (org metrics 86.2/84.0 precision/recall); MacCormack 2012 (2.3–6.1x propagation cost for tightly-coupled orgs). **[verified]** — [Nagappan](https://www.cs.umd.edu/users/basili/publications/proceedings/P125.pdf); [MacCormack](https://dash.harvard.edu/bitstream/handle/1/34403525/maccormack,baldwin,rusnak_exploring-the-duality.pdf)
- Market does not punish accretion: adoption driven by libraries and existing code (Meyerovich & Rabkin 2013). **[verified via summary]** — [Haldar summary](https://vivekhaldar.com/articles/empirical-analysis-of-programming-language-adoption)
- Debt is architectural: 54% top-3 / 73% of examples (Ernst 2015). **[verified]** — [Ernst 2015](https://cs.unibg.it/esecfse_proceedings/fse15/p50-ernst.pdf)
- RCE's own rule: the model proposes, the check admits (§5.5). **[verified]** — [RCE paper](https://research.openie.dev/papers/rce/)

### Inferences (the structural rules, stated for RCE governance)
1. **Admission is a check, not a vote.** Every small-language case replaced reviewer enthusiasm with a rule (10:1, composition, freeze). RCE already says the check admits equivalences, not the model; the same should hold for spec changes: a proposed construct is admitted only if it passes a stated mechanical criterion (e.g., not expressible as composition of existing mechanisms; measured benefit over a stated harm threshold). This converts the Stroustrup asymmetry (enthusiasm cheap, opposition expensive) into its inverse.
2. **Composition before addition, with the cost named.** Lua's rule kept the core at 25k lines but produced incompatible user-level libraries. If RCE adopts the rule, the spec should say where the incompatibility is allowed to live (outside the gate) and where it is not (inside).
3. **Symmetric removal.** WG21's "nothing significant goes away" plus "don't break existing code" is the accretion ratchet. A spec that cannot remove will only grow; Lehman II holds unless work reduces complexity. RCE should pair every admission with a removal path, or freeze (Elm) and move evolution to the ecosystem.
4. **One decision locus per interface.** Nagappan's result is that binaries touched by many org units fail; Conway's is that each org boundary becomes an interface. A spec governed by a committee whose membership boundary runs through the language is, by the Vista data, a predicted defect site. Keep the number of bodies that can write to the spec at one (or a tiny fixed set), and route everyone else through the proposal/decline process with published reasons, as Go does (the Go reasons taxonomy — feasibility, limited use, breaking principles, existing alternatives — is reusable as a decline rubric).
5. **Do not rely on the market to prune.** Meyerovich & Rabkin show adoption tracks existing code and libraries, not language quality; bloat is not selected against on the timescale it accrues. Smallness must be enforced by the governance rule, not expected from competition.
6. **Measure the gate.** The Go study gives a baseline (41% declined, median 31–37 days, 15% resubmission). RCE's spec governance could publish the same metrics so the decline bias is observable rather than asserted; and propagation cost (MacCormack) or org-intersection (Nagappan) can be computed on RCE's own repositories as the falsifier for "handoffs are where failures occur."
7. **AI changes the rate, not the rule.** The GitClear trend (duplication up, refactoring down) is what a human-reviewed gate does at higher generation rates. The admission check must be mechanical to scale with model output; otherwise the model becomes the dominant source of Lehman-II growth.

### Gaps
- RCE §7 (governance) was not readable in this session; these rules may duplicate or contradict what §7 already says. The report writer should reconcile.
- No source quantifies the defect or complexity effect of language freezes (Elm) vs decline-biased evolution (Go) vs committee accretion (C++); the comparison above is structural, not measured.
- The "Glasgow 2014" manual-page study and Scala/Swift feature-growth critiques were not found and remain unsupported.
